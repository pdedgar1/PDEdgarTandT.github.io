I began preparing to answer the first question posed in this exam when I first enrolled in the Texts and Technology program, and folks began to ask “What does that mean?” This question and the subsequent “but what does it mean to you?” pose that Texts and Technology is less predominantly a subject area than a mode of thought made possible (to me) by the multivalence of the word “means.” While in her book What’s the Use?, Sara Ahmed traces the valence of the word use across culture and distinguishes ‘use’ from ‘meaning’ (“What a word means is its use,” a la Wittgenstein (1958, in Ahmed 2019, p. 49), I want to honor an ethos Ahmed expresses when she says “instrumentality and affection can be part of the same story” (2019, p. 138) and intentionally collapse ‘use’ and ‘meaning’ together. What poetry ‘means’ is what it makes possible—a way of seeing, understanding, expressing, and being—and this is at the heart of both ‘technology’ and ‘meaning’: to ‘mean’ something affords the world something, and ‘texts’ and ‘technologies’ are by extension means to affective, economic, disciplinary, intellectual ends. 
Saying ‘Texts and Technology’ frames a technology (text-technologies) with and against technology as a concept. In this essay, I’ll (1) discuss the kind of thinking that affords for T&T transdisciplinary work to exist, and (2) discuss what that transdisciplinary perspective of T&T work means for poetry and periodical studies. While, in 2026, ‘technology’ could  function as/mean a cultural shorthand for ‘smartphones and social media,’ I choose to look back upon the histories and disciplines in which we are trained—literature, gameplay, poetry, computation, etc.—and to conceive of them all as conversant configurations of technologies of practice. As a scholar of poetry across media, I’m particularly attentive to this question of what poetry as a technology affords or means when it appears in various iterations: in ancient and spiritual texts, in mass-market/avant-garde periodicals, and on social media and in LLM training data/chatlogs. 
By what means can we think across disciplines?
The framing ‘texts and technology’ means/affords a way of seeing our (T&T students’) respective traditions as being bootstrapped from the most ancient material and conceptual technologies. The content generated by participants of this program (e.g., the Timeline Project in History of T&T) means something, allowing each scholar to develop their thoughts, designs, revisions, innovations, and critiques in their fields. I ground this way of thinking about disciplinarity itself in language that emerged among contemporaries. Thomas S. Kuhn and Michel Foucault, who between 1964 and 1966 published The Structure of Scientific Revolutions and The Order of Things, respectively, describe similar sociological phenomena: that a paradigm or an episteme at a moment in time, makes possible certain questions, actions, etc. Kuhn says, “one of the things a scientific community acquires with a paradigm is a criterion for choosing problems that, while the paradigm is taken for granted, can be assumed to have solutions” (1964, p. 37). When Kuhn describes the relationship between “the paradigm shift” and “normal science” as a puzzle-solving work (1964, p. 35), he foreshadows Wendy Hui Kyong Chun’s language for the tensions of habitual new media, which boils down to “Habit + Crisis = Update” (2024, p. 63). Kuhn’s concept the paradigm [shift] centers on scientific activity which, in one example, “reformulate[s] mechanical theory in an equivalent but logically and aesthetically more satisfying form” (1964, p. 33, emphasis mine on the conjunction of use and meaning), pointing to the enmeshment of scientific concepts with the human experience of those frameworks. 
The human experience of uses of scientific frameworks is not just affective for the scientific community, but as Foucault centers in The Order of Things, those frameworks are fleshed out into material and social technologies. While Kuhn takes the point of view of the workers in scientific disciplines, Foucault takes “the point of view of the rules that come into play in the very existence of [scientific] discourse” (1966, p. xiv) to “reveal a positive unconscious of knowledge” (p. xi, emphasis his). Foucault, recognizing that “science” as a field of inquiry bootstraps not just from mathematics and observation and science but from language and text, positions the science practices of a period as discourses; the language of “paradigm shift” becomes in his work “discontinuities in the episteme” (p. xxiv), which are traceable not just through reconfigurations of technologies of practice in the scientific sense (as Kuhn shows by examples in chemistry), but also in discourses across time. The move from Classical thought to modern thought, Foucault says, is caught up not just in scientific disciplines, but in the language of science moving from discourses of resemblance and “similitudes” (1966, p. 19) to the discourses of the Other, of “discontinuity” (p. 235). These discursive movements are signs of change and effect it, transforming the Western senses of ‘labor’ in an economy, of ‘humans’ with ‘organic structure,’ and the power of ‘representation’ (p. 257, 259). Foucault’s historiography in The Order of Things can be rightly challenged, but the principles I glean from the work, that discourses are both texts with baked-in traces of past conceptual technologies and conceptual technologies that mean/afford something to those who carry it forward in time, have been fleshed out by other works I will discuss below in this and the next essay. 
While systems of thought enable certain sciences and technologies to come into being, these conceptual and scientific technologies mean or afford restructurings of societal power. For example, Thomas J. Misa’s Leonardo to the Internet discusses the technological and societal shifts that transitioned global economies, empires, and languages on the basis of the technologies developed in that time— it might be retitled The Sociology of Scientific Revolutions. Misa starts tracking “Technologies of the Court” not the technologies Leonardo Da Vinci developed for the Medici court, the French court, etc., and in the court as a technology itself that by its structure moves power and money to courtly ends: “courts that employed Leonardo and his fellow engineers…. focused the era’s technologists on warfare, city building, courtly entertainment, and dynastic display” (p. 12), not on commerce as the mercantile technologies of the Dutch East Trading Company did (pp. 40-28) or on industry as the manufacturing technology of the city of Manchester’s “factory system” did (p. 87). The social systems themselves depended on bootstrapping multiple technologies in time—which, for example, required and precipitated changes in the gendered, mechanical, and locational dimensions of cotton-spinning (from skilled women’s home labor on jennies to unskilled factory women’s supervision of mule-driven spinning machines, pp. 72-74). To close the loop, what Misa also helps us see throughout Leonardo to the Internet is that the incidents of technological formation precipitate meaningful (re: powerful, activating) technologies of critique. When Manchester emerges as a site of industrial technology, its situation both inspires Friedrich Engels to write on The Conditions of the Working Class in England and finances (as Engels was full partner in industry there) Karl Marx’s writing of Das Kapital (p. 78-9). Technologies and texts make each other possible, over and over and over, up and into the Internet age. 
While the titular framing of Misa’s book bounds his work’s timeline, its subjects remind us that as much as our academic disciplines may be professionally bounded, the world we live in is not. Leonardo da Vinci, poster child for the term “Renaissance man,” was an interdisciplinary scholar with talents in mathematics, anatomy, physics, aviation, religion, and politics, and the patronage system, Misa describes, patronized his various services. Neither does networked Internet media bind our data up by discipline, try as we might to cultivate a “brand” online.
Here, I turn from the way technologies of thought are related to social systems to the way they are related to writing technologies, the latter changing the former. Though we invent new languages to program in, language as an organizing communicative protocol are bootstrapped into nearly all human endeavors, and according to Walter Ong, those communicative protocols structure how we think. In Orality and Literacy he lays out how the mediatization of language into script, into text, helped global cultures re-structure thought in unique ways: textual composition “gives thought different contours from those of orally sustained thought” (Ong 1982, p. 129). Much of Ong’s work traces how even the “textual” emerges in a series of stages bootstrapped from technologies like the stylus, the alphabet, and printing. The configuration of more text-technologies for handling written information is the subject of Daniel Headrick’s When Information Came of Age, which traces the period when “information” and “data” as a collectible began to influence scientific and sociopolitical thinking. “The supply of information never ‘satisfies’ demand,” he says, “increasing amount of information, in turn, elicited innovations in the methods of handling it— in other words, information systems. Hence the development of scientific taxonomy, cartography, lexicography, statistics, and postal services” (Headrick 2000, p. 11). To take this identification of information structures a step further, digital remediation, Jay David Bolter proposed in Writing Space, also restructures thought in its own ways, because “in hypertext, displacement becomes the customary rhetorical strategy, whereas consecutive, choronological order is the exception” (2001, p. 137). Hypertext is as much a rhetorical technology as an electronic technology, to Bolter and other new media theorists, because cyberspace “is an expression and an extension of both” the natural world and the social world (Bolter 2001, p. 98). As an interdisciplinary scholar, speaking, writing, and digitizing my work helps me think about my own endeavors in unique ways. My poetry [research] is embodied and performed and discussed differently than it is written, differently than it is digitized, and the boundaries between these slip—I speak, write, and “scroll” differently.
What interdisciplinary means (technologies) bound my work? 
Poetry is notoriously difficult to account for—for the record, my working definition as a writer is “communication that demands a level of attention to language smaller than the word.” [i] And if the meaning of a technology like poetry is found in what it affords, it contains what its designer intends and what its users make of it. Contrary to Barthes, who says that “the rise of the reader comes at the expense of the death of the author,” poetry as a technology allows both its writer and its reader to pay not just close attention to the arrangement of expressions of perceived experiences (which we now call information or data), but to the experiences themselves networked in this thing called life, to our accounts of things. What separates the technology of language from the technology of poetry are what poems afford that language itself does not. By these logics, poetry is a made thing, a technology dependent on the premises and material of language. With language as a raw material (Barthes notes, in “From Work To Text,” that “etymologically, the text is a tissue, a woven fabric”), we can move forward with it as any other resource and assume construction with such material might be technologized further. Language and poetry are two of our oldest technologies, and while language undergirds our ability to perform almost every other, my observation and research interest lies in the fact that poetry, buoyant and transmissible, sticky and affective, always surfaces atop new media. 
By new media, I mean any media at the time of its emergence—contrary to Wendy Hui Kyong Chun, who seems to use new media as shorthand for “Web2.0.” Though Chun uses the term ‘new media’ narrowly, the applicative force of her argument about ‘habitual new media’ extends into the deep past. How far back does the sense of Updating to Remain the Same extend, only to an Instapoet’s account? To the penny presses, who parallel social media platforms insofar as they juxtapose sensational stories, humor, entertainment with heavy geopolitical and economic news? To Headrick’s descriptions of Encyclopedias and Dictionaries, which expanded to accommodate a growing body of knowledge (p. 153)? To the circuit riders, bards and troubadours, and Greek rhetors who circled regions iterating the same stories as they delivered them to each town? To ask this question requires us to be able to see periodicity and repetition as distinct technologies that are configured for different uses. For Walter Ong, repetitions of “epithetic formulas ‘wise Nestor’ or ‘clever Odysseus’” (p. 76) are mnemonics, technologies of memory, for the oral poet; Bolter notes that the repeated printing of a text is how pre-digital culture accounted for the prestige, the “stability, monumentality, and authority” of a text (Bolter, p. 162-3); for Chun, repetition as habit is “central to grasping the paradoxes of new media” (p. 15, emphasis mine). To me, the periodical supposes the future of a way of publishing, of numbering a publication “1”—or “0”— in anticipation of the next, even if the next never arrives (as is the case with literary magazines and social media accounts alike. 
Any claim to drawing these parallels is and must remain an interdisciplinary undertaking: not only that I should have the theoretical chops to see the similarities across media, but the disciplinary expertise and/or collaborative methodology that allows one to see the differences between media. The shared ethic of ephemerality between a little magazine and electronic literature, or the confessional bents of poetry both in Ms. magazine and on Instapoetry, do not make them the same, and it’s our multiliteracies that reveal to us the ways in which the conceptual and material bootstraps of those poetics are similar yet different. What these differences mean—what various media afford to those who make poetry, who read it, who doula it into the world—is at the heart of my historical, critical, archival, performed, played, and prototyped research, creative, and editorial work. 
To bootstrap language from Barthes once more, the field of Texts and Technology “is an irreducible plural”; “it answers not to an interpretation, even a liberal one, but to an explosion, a dissemination” (159). My research into periodicals and poetics depends on computational and networked archives available to me, content-consumption habits I have that keep me informed about ongoing literary production, enrollment at 20th-century institutions that in the 21st century allow for dual-degrees and triple-majoring, and the episteme of hypermediated, self-reflexive, deconstructed poetics and critique, not to make little of the personal life and financial circumstances that brought me here, that meant that I could afford to attend UCF. And this is only my beginning. ‘Texts and Technology’ means to read everything once as a text and once as functional, potential, systematic, once as material and once as conceptual, once by computer and once in print, and then—maybe once more. 
 

 
Generative AI technologies are perhaps the ultimate bootstrapped text-technology, built out of statistical modeling, Big Data collection and cloud computing, and chat-like interfaces with other multimodalities like text-to-image, text-to-code-to-command line, and others. And yet, to use Janet Murray’s language opening the New Media Reader, GenAI, as I will refer to it henceforth, is an “expressive medium” bootstrapped upon the “digital medium” among its multiple forms, which itself was “formed by the braided interplay of technical invention and cultural expression” (2003, p. 3). In my previous essay, I asserted that both material and conceptual technologies afford certain ways of seeing, being, and making, and that the fields and disciplines in which we are engaged are all bootstrapped in different ways from the same text-technologies (for example: writing, print/digital publishing), and technology as a concept, in conversant configurations of technologies of practice. In a program like Texts and Technologies, “interdisciplinarity” functions not just as methodology or concept, but as the epistemological premise that understands “no technology is entirely new” and “information, individuals, and systems can be related to one another because they are related”—including Generative AI. The New Media Reader is a primary expression of the T&T epistemology-in-practice, because Noah Wardrip-Fruin and Nick Montfort, its editors, actually approach the text-technology of the “anthology” as a piece of networked media, reflecting in its print design “Link Lines,” “symbols and devices intended to aid in navigating,” reappropriating the technology of the hyperlink (an image-text-action) back into print (p. xiv). The previous essay also hinted at a uniting thread that has gone unstated so far but is crucial to the perspective that Texts and Technology, and poetry specifically, bring to bear upon our research in the world: the ethical dimension. 
Because new media are not just meaningful in terms of their use-value, but meaningful in the affective sense of the word, they like any new technology generate in us frictions and tensions, anxieties and fears that result in calculated choices that we experience as morally-grounded—Generative AI being one of the most salient case-examples of this decade. In this essay, I’d like to lay out how the texts in the New Media Reader, Distributed Blackness, Digital Black Feminism, Race After Technology, Design Justice, and Cultural Analytics equip digital humanists, Texts-and-Technologists, poets, and publishers to approach the field of computational creativity. Before I begin, let us tie a small ethical string around our finger: when we follow the bootstraps of all present-day digital technology all the way down, we find that the soles of the boot, so to say, are pressed into the faces of exploited workers and endangered life forms around the globe. The texts on the core list prepare us to deal with more than Generative AI—the technologies of “Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code” (Benjamin 2019), of “Design Justice” (Costanza-Chock 2020), and methodological technologies for research like “cultural analytics,” (Manovich 2020), and Critical Technocultural Discourse Analysis (Brock, 2020), all enable us to see much further down than is comfortable to admit. (As a final aside, poetry does too). 
The Physical Equipment of GenAI.
To begin my approach to GenAI, it is helpful to break down its components into discrete technologies that we can trace back before the language of “generative AI” entered the discourse. I must do this for the sake of time and focus, because, as Murray notes, the first author in the NMR is Jorge Luis Borges, who “is fascinated by the arbitrariness of language itself” (2000, p. 3). Language’s arbitrariness is a grounding T&T epistemology that allows us at one time to consider that what “GenAI” means as a technology product and what it means as a social-economic-political phenomenon, which overlap but are not equivalent. Andre Brock’s language at the end of his book Distributed Blackness, about the libidinal economy of Black Twitter and Black technoculture, parallels this multivalence. Brock says, 
‘Technology as text’ has multiple postulations for distributed Blackness and Black technoculture: 
·      code (interfaces and practices)
·      the digitally distributed content generated by and mediated by that code 
·      signifyin’ and other cultural discourses of Black digital practitioners. (2020, p. 211)
As a technology, GenAI holds that language exists in mappable high-dimensional space, and that the output of a model is not fixed, but contingent on a configuration of interlocked technologies like (1) a Big Data training set, (2) a data architecture, and (3) a statistical model housed in (4) hundreds of thousands of computer chips in warehouses. As a phenomenon, GenAI is (5) an object of discourse, (6) a marketed product, (7) an interactive medium with a GUI, (8) a [dys/u]topian ideal, and (9) an affective virtual personality. Maybe it’s more things. But all said, GenAI means a lot of things—not just much, but many; “GenAI” is a disturbingly reductive term for a hyperobject that the consumes power, water, and information from across the world. 
Being able to break a phenomenon like AI into component parts like this is an act of design thinking, and doing so for the purposes of critique brings us up to Sasha Costanza-Chock uses in Design Justice. “Popular narratives of design, technology, and social change,” she says, “are dominated by techno-utopian hype about ever-more-powerful personal devices, “intelligent” systems” (2020, p. xvii). Costanza-Chock defines design as “plan,” “pattern,” “composition,” “shape, appearance, or features of an object,” (p. 13) but being derived from the latin “to mark out” (p. 12), she retains subjectivity to the processes of design and critique, in the tradition of Donna Haraway, who calls for transparency of “situated knowledges”: “Struggles over what will count as rational accounts of the world are struggles over how to see,” she says, “how to see from below is a problem requiring at least as much skill with bodies and language, with the mediations of vision, as the ‘highest’ techno-scientific visualizations”; (1991, p. 194, emphasis hers). The project of deciding what GenAI will look like in our institutions, in our personal practices, in our communities, and in the future, is a design process, one that requires that we speak from our situations, skillfully. I believe we can. In the next two sections, I’ll layout the affordances of certain T&T texts for thinking about the technological and social components of AI. 
How do T&T Texts make thinking about AI as a computer technology possible? 
The “Texts and Technology” methodologies and epistemologies inherently center multiplicity of viewpoint. In Cultural Analytics, Lev Manovich describes how we as researchers can use large datasets and computers to perform research tasks, but emphasizes “you need to know the core ideas and principles that make such operations possible…. Thus, it teaches you how you can explore cultural datasets yourself and also explains how our society thinks using data” (2020, p. 2). Capture is a helpful thing, like the “screen captures” in Design Justice and other works, but Catherine Knight Steele also describes how capture is an epistemology of slavery as well as a potential praxis for Black Feminist storytelling, whether our own story or others (2020, p. 102-103). Benjamin describes how the Polaroid and Kodak systems were calibrated to capture white faces, not Black faces, exposing difference” (2019, p. 106-8). In contrast to perspective drawing, which might play a certain “God Trick” (Haraway) or premise a certain cultural way of seeing (Foucault uses multiperspectival perspective painting as the opening image in The Order of Things), methods like Brock’s Critical Technocultural Discourse Analysis have been instrumental and dear to my approaches to periodical studies, poetry, and AI. CTDA, Brock says, “requires assembly,” applying a chosen “critical discourse analytic to the interface and to discourses about the interface” (2020, p. 43). Critical Digital Black Feminist and New Media theories are perfectly applicable to our GenAI phenomenon, especially as it plays out, as I discussed earlier and lay out below, as both a technology, interface, experience, phenomenon, and discourse. 
(1) AI as Big Data training set. The origins of AI are myriad, and the reality is that the training data that makes up AI is “captured” from the Internet and other networked technologies, like video cameras, government data, videos and fanfiction, any scrapeable media that was ever online and not protected. “Vulnerability” as a Millennial moral became “vulnerability” in a cybersecurity sense, and the language of how this information and data are “captured reappears over and over in the literature of critical information scholarship as they describe how algorithms see the world and act on it. “Every time you capture a photo, the phone camera algorithms automatically choose the exposure and adjust the contrast of the photo and also identify the type of scene and objects in the photo,” Manovich describes in Cultural Analytics (2020). 
(2) AI as a data architecture. Algorithms, the basis for GenAI, are ways of structuring data as predictive. Prediction is an ancient biological technology baked into our experiences and instincts, and it’s also perceived as a sociological and/or religious technology, the ability to foresee events, tragedies, wars, consequences, to soothsay. While useful, Ruha Benjamin recalls Cathy O’Neil’s words: “[r]acism is the most slovenly of predictive models. It is powered by haphazard data gathering and spurious correlations” (12). Benjamin’s book Race After Technology poses the language of “the New Jim Code” as the use of new technologies in ways that [un]intentionally perpetuate discriminations. Race and racism are technologies, Benjamin says, “designed to separate, stratify, and sanctify the many forms of injustice experienced by members of racialized groups, but [that] people routinely reimagine and redeploy to their own ends” (33). Because these architectures are designed purposes in mind, they [dis/dys]afford certain actions and behaviors, as Sasha Costanza-Chock describes in Design Justice. Those affordances are [un]available and [im]perceptible to people based on their standpoints (p. 38).  
(3) AI as statistical models – Algorithms, like race, are dependent on pre-existing institutional structures and datasets bootstrapped on other preexisting technologies, like legal and political permissions, designers’ motives (e.g., not-for-profit vs. for-profit motives), resource and human exploitation, and cultural narratives around technology like inevitability. Benjamin’s book argues that because “Codes are both reflective and predictive” (p. 12). Computer code as a text-technology makes up our world, in the sense that computer codes make our digital worlds possible and fictionalize it, only feeding the computer information deemed relevant by the programmer.
(4) AI as computer chips in warehouses. The earlier texts in the NMR remind us that all computers, even the computers we speak to on the Internet, weren’t just ‘at one time in the past’ physical, but still are. Furthermore, every computer technology we now take for granted was once an idea that someone wrote down on a piece of paper, imagined both in the conceptual space and as a physical machine that might be built in the near future, iterating on each other from the Memex (Bush, p. 44) to project Xanadu in Computer Lib/Dream Machines that imagines the best possible version of networked text technologies. The descriptions of media art exhibits in the anthology, like Kaprow et al.’s “Happenings,” Ted Nelson et al.’s “Software—Information Technology: It’s New Meaning For Art,” Myron Krueger’s “Responsive Environments” describe and picture for the reader the hardware at work in making media art available to the public, reminding us that there is work going on behind the scenes, opening up the potential that we might ask, “where is the work behind the scenes happening to make each technology of the AI age happen?” Who collects the data? Who tags it? Who structures it? Who maintains Claude in a moment of breakdown? Who is accountable when something goes wrong? 
How do T&T texts make thinking about AI as a social technology possible? 
(6) AI as a marketed product. Catherine Knight Steele’s work on Digital Black Feminism ends with a prescient chapter on what happens when an effective and affective technology like Digtial Black Womanhood, being, and identity end up being commodified. “Digital platforms can package the ideas of Black feminism for sale,” she says, (Steele, 2020, p. 134), and once the ideas can be commodified, so can the products of its practitioners and the products of those who would capitalize on images of Blackness though they themselves are not. Steele’s work provides us with a critical lens to view the ways that characterizations, especially the “voice” and “code-switching” abilities of GenAI, Character.AI, and AI Deepfakes, traffic in the affects, language, and images of women and identity groups. 
(7) AI as an interactive medium with a GUI. Similarly to section (4), the New Media Reader portrays the development of the individual parts of the computer interface we receive when we purchase a laptop not as whole, but as individuated expressions of a certain desire for a specific way of thinking with computers. Douglas Engelbart describes wanting to be able to link Cards together in a computer system to “Augment Human Intellect,” as a function of the computer; “we do not speak of isolated clever tricks that help in particular situations” (1962, p. 95). In the subsequent chapter, the literal programming of a graphical communication system in Sketchpad is presented by Ivan Sutherland as an alternative to having to continually (slowly) type out commands (1963, p. 111). The design processes for the computers we write with now, and indeed, the design processes for AI, are born[e] incrementally by individuals with certain desires for speed in action, for “Universal Publishing” (Nelson, 1981, p. 443). As with the material nature of GenAI systems, the structural technologies at work in the interface of AI, the Chats and discussions of “alignment” that focus less on how personalized the bot sounds than on whether it will accidentally suggest something evil, are all discrete decisions and processes that can be observed by critical media scholars. 
(8) AI as a [dys/u]topian ideal. There are many entrypoints with which to frame the apocalypse that seems to characterize the affective importance of “AI” phenomenologically. Seeing as I have already addressed GenAI as marketing, I want to step back to a theoretical reading that for me echoes some of the movements of GenAI, to Deleuze and Guattari’s language about the book and the co-authored text. Their selections in the NMR contain language reminiscent of GenAI debates in both tone and use: while talking about their co-authored book Anti-Oedipus, they assert they wrote it together (more than the two of them) with all the information in their range, “To reach, not the point where one no longer says I, but the point where it is no longer of any importance whether one says I. We are no longer ourselves…. We have been aided, inspired, multiplied.” The authors’ hyper-intertextual understanding of the book is as an assemblage of language, a multiplicity, a “body without organs,” an abstract machine measured by quantified writing, a rhizome. Such language opens up, or threatens, the end of intellectual property, opens up, or threatens, an assumption of connectivity between any two things in dimensional space that has always only a middle (Deleuze and Guattari, p. 408-9). This thought experiment, this way of thinking, is not just ideology in the age of AI, but “antigeneology,” “short-term memory, or antimemory,” that “operates by variation, expansion, conquest, capture, offshoots” (p. 409): the very operational logics of GenAI companies and of the potentials that undergird both large language models and its enabler, technocapitalism. 
(9) AI as an affective virtual personality. It’s nearly impossible to think about the way that GenAI is playing out in the social sphere without recalling ELIZA, “a computer program with which one could converse,” as Joseph Weizenbaum’s cautionary tale is laid out to us in the NMR (excerpted from Computer Power and Human Reason, p. 369): “I was startled to see how quickly and how very deeply people conversing with DOCTOR became emotionally involved with the computer,” he says. Weizenbaum, as a computer scientist, knows that the program which he developed is made only of code, metals, plastics, and electricity, but what his experience revealed is what other media theorists call immediacy: that intelligent people make attributions of humanity to information communication, especially when the materialities of the mediation is made invisible to them, but continue even despite when the designs of the mediation are made visible to them. That technological “spirit of the times” makes even science seem to Weizenbaum to be an “illusory system,” merely matters of failures of communication, certainties suspensions of disbelief (p. 373-5), but to those of us studying GenAI and society, the appearance of “science as an addictive drug” (p. 374) positions us to be critical, to keep inquiring. 
Ruha Benjamin cites Safiya Noble’s words in Algorithms of Oppression in Race After Technology: “an app will not save us” (Noble 2018, p. 165), but presuming that designing new, or differently, is the solution prompts Benjamin to ask “have we even agreed upon the problem?” (p. 168). Benjamin proposes coded equity audits as one abolitionist tool in the tradition of danah boyd and M. C. Elish: 
·      What are the unintended consequences of designing systems at scale on the basis of existing patterns in society?
·      When and how should AI systems prioritize individuals over society and vice versa?
·      When is introducing an AI system the right answer – and when is it not? (Benjamin 2019, p. 172-3). 
 
But given that the companies implementing Gen AI systems we have been given seem to only be asking these questions in hindsight, I want to conclude by returning to the interdisciplinary ideal of Texts & Technology, as well as to the work of poetry as a formational technology that floats to the top of the many uses of technology. 
            The interdisciplinary epistemology of Texts and Technology supposes that our conceptual frameworks are no ornamental object, the next layer of a sandcastle, but are and are to be used out in the world. Benjamin’s work proposes Tools for the New Jim Code; Catherine 
Knight Steele’s book considers Black Feminist Praxis; Costanza-Chock advocates for design justice; Cultural Analytics proposes we use the capacities available on our computers to do important investigative and expressive work. Remember the string we tied on our fingers? Poetry and good critical writing enable us to push technologies to the places where they reveal their limits. Expressive and algorithmic media, these networks that connect the empire to us, connect our governments to us, connect the world’s resources to our doorsteps, also connect us to the empire, to our governments, to the world. There is a tug of war, and we need researchers and writers across disciplines to pick up the rope. The New Media Reader, like the critical frameworks that make up a significant portion of the T&T reading list, provide an epistemology that sees how we might be connected to one another, and how that connects us to a better world we design together. 

 
In my first exam, I wrote about the role of texts and technology as a way of seeing the world as meaning-full. Meaning for the T&T student, I wrote, is about what new media afford what they make conceptually or literally possible. In other words, meaning is found in what we make (emphasis on production) from what we understand (emphasis on multiliteracy). My study of little magazines, takes this premise literally: I make and investigate little magazines along the lines of my current and embodied understanding of little magazines. In this essay, I argue that recent methodologies that have been proposed to investigate little magazines are expressions of an understanding of the little magazine indebted to Hoffman, Allen, and Ulrich’s The Little Magazine: A History and a Bibliography, except for one: the lens of publication technology. To start, I will trace out the formation of four distinct methodologies disciplines use to study the little magazine. At the end of each quarter, I will describe what unifies the field of little magazine studies, as a subsection of periodical studies: the at-risk-ness of litmags, their most defining feature, that also impacts each vein of inquiry because of the way it implicates the means of production. In my conception, however, the precarity of the litmag is not exclusive to avant-garde work, but a feature of any aesthetic production dependent on publishing technology. 
Come to The Well 
Before breaking off to speak to the different elements of our understanding of little magazines now, I will start by laying out how the literary magazine has been traditionally understood, giving Hoffman, Allen, and Ulrich their due. This is not my effort to try and combine two essay questions together, because I will eschew what the little magazine means to me. Rather, one’s emphasis or interest in a strain of characterization of little magazines has a reflexive impact on the practice of research into that strain. I will spend much of this essay tracing the lineage of thought downstream from Hoffman, Allen, and Ulrich’s The Little Magazine to the major contemporary interventions into the methodology of little magazine research. very definition of the little magazine. I will refer to little magazines as ‘litmags’ from now on as an abbreviation, a nod to avant-garde poetics, and a nod to the ‘little’ in ‘little magazines’ that separates them from mass media organizations and institutions. When I refer to broader literary culture, like the literary reviews and critical journals, I will refer to them in full. 
A “litmag” is this media genre that Hoffman et al. define as “designed to print artistic work that for reasons of commercial expediency is not acceptable to the money-minded periodicals or press” (Hoffman et al., 1947 p. 2). Hoffman et al. see the little magazine as editors’ attempts to discover and promote the advance guard at any, expense, often personal expense (pp. 218). Across the board, The Little Magazine finds the litmag to be a scrappy, hustling genre that seeks out new writers with new work, often invoking the sentiment that the writing they feature is unpublishable anywhere else. Sometimes, editors are observers of movements who wish to give them a home or a vessel; sometimes, editors are members of larger political and critical movements seeking to foster work that will make an impact. As the litmag solidifies as the main organ of the modernist project, personalities emerge; they network with one another, and the form of the litmag takes hold in more circles than just the Imagists: a litmag represents a collection of people, often far-flung, who share similar visions for poetry, fiction, and so on. Hoffman et al.’s work has been raw material from which five major characterizations have been spun: Litmags are (1) editorial projects for (2) avant-garde literature that (3) collects and binds works representative of a movement or time, (4) artists and authors together, (5) often at a loss.
A sense for litmags’ at-riskness is congruent with Bourdieu’s placement of the little review in a corner of his “Field of Cultural Production” defined by high autonomy, a very small audience, and little-to-no economic profit (Bourdieu & Johnson, 1993, p. 49). Crucially, there is almost no market for the litmag except for the community of writers, editors, and publishers. This fifth characterization does not map cleanly into Table 1 below, because every act of intellectual production investigating the litmag opposes the precarity principle. To record the memories of editors, to wrestle with the experiments they published and preserve them, to refer to them as artifacts of an important moment in time, as evidence of and fostering communities of writers, flies in the face of their death.

Though Hoffman et al. define the litmag clearly in The Little Magazine, the structure of their text is worth noting so that I can make one final opening point. Like many of the volumes I will discuss in this essay, Hoffman et al. balance a discussion of the litmag as a formal artifact with its tension in society, with the way litmags play out. While they spend the first third of their book using the case studies of Poetry, Others, and the Little Review to solidify the defining characteristics of the little magazine, they spend the middle third of the book pulling out trends and themes in publishing: regionalism, political writing, psychoanalysis, and philosophy and criticism. Some themes that Hoffman et al. hint towards, such as university publishing (“Many of the eclectic magazines.… often reflect the tastes and preoccupations of the university community in which they originate”, p. 9) are identified as major trends in later collections of reflections on the litmag. The themes listed above—location, politics, form, and theory— become binding concepts for researchers wading through swaths of periodicals, separate from what defines litmags themselves. 
There is one last binding concept for researchers today that we don’t receive from Hoffman et al. directly: publication technology. Their stories of struggling magazines do depend on communication and publication technologies, like the US Post and the printing press. The Post Office burned several runs of Ulysses in Margaret Anderson’s The Little Review; when Harriet Monroe doubled the page count in Poetry, the cost increased to $13,000 annually (almost $300,000 today!), which she struggled to cover with limited grants, subscriptions, and gifts. When he secured Man Ray’s employer’s printing press as a gift and it broke in the move, Alfred Kreymborg made a deal with the owners of the Washington Square Bookshop to publish Glebe, one of the first experimental and imagist-featuring litmags in the US, until “the controlling pocketbook gradually usurped power” (Hoffman et al., 1947, pp. 59; 43-46). But the authors never spend as much ink on the literal making of a journal as they do on the social, economic, and political situations those technologies connote. Most major works since then, however, do. In the following four sections, I will trace out the how Hoffman et al’s characteristics of the litmag (Litmags as Editorial Projects, their Avant-Garde Qualities, as Time Capsules, and as Evidence of Communities in-Progress) have defined the ways we study litmags since then. Critically, I will describe how the litmags’ precarity is contingent on the deaths of each of these threads, and how these deaths are contingent on publication technologies. 
Editorial Projects  
The first major characterization of the litmag has I’m going to review is the magazine as a project of passionate editors, which is fleshed out in two major ways in the study of literary magazines. In the histories of literary magazines, certain editors show up over and over in ways that suggest celebrity status (Ezra Pound and T.S. Eliot, for example). In “The Role of the Periodical Editor,” Matthew Philpotts argues that attunement to the editor and their lore has often left their real success unmeasured, and the concept of litmag editorship undertheorized. To remedy this, he appeals to Bourdieu’s notion of habitus to build out a theory of the little magazine editor as both a character and a professional: editorial habitus, he says, “mediates actively between the charismatic and bureaucratic modes of editorship” (Philpotts, 2012, p. 57). T.S. Eliot’s editorship rests on a set of principles; it’s a project that Philpotts says can be narrowed down to (i) introducing new writers’ work, (ii) bringing ideological extremes together, and (iii) creating a trans-European “site for fruitful intellectual exchange” (p. 58). For Philpotts, being able to triangulate the editorial habitus of “the editor, the periodical, and the field” in such a way that we could measure their successes and gesture at why some magazines succeed and last, and others close quickly after their inaugural issue (p. 62). Research that prioritizes mapping a field of litmags at a given moment in time often prioritizes the editors’ own voices over such measurements. Each chapter in some of the works I will mention in the next paragraph solidifies as a monument to the existence of a magazine, and to the attempt of the editor at that time, overshadowing some of the impacts that cultural production had. (Though her work is not on this list, Sarah RudeWalker's Revolutionary Poetics actually does this).  
However, when composing a media history of the litmag, The Little Magazine in America: A Documentary History, WithoutCovers://Literary_magazinesthe_digital_edge, and The Little Magazine in Contemporary America do a standout job of revealing what anxieties editors had in 1978, 2002, and 2015, respectively, in profound moments of social and technological change. The anthology as a methodology of study for the little magazine gathers dozens of editors of wide-ranging journals into three collections, and enable the identification of multiple editorial habitus in each of these moments. Across these editions, we watch the Paris Review turn from an exile magazine into a major literary powerhouse, see critical university magazines grow and evolve, hear as several very different avant-gardes justify themselves in their own words, and watch as the production methods of various literary magazines diversify. 
The at-riskness of the litmag in these books is in the death of the authors and editors that were featured in the magazines. When editorial habitus is the driver of a publication, the end of an editor or their will is the end of a litmag. The limitations of the methodology that involves anthologizing editors, then, is a move against the death of their expertise, their wisdom, and their vision for litmags and poetry and fiction and nonfiction. Editors don’t remember things perfectly, and are so close to the work as to not be able to see past its pages to its generational impact, but I was struck when reading how transferable their notes are into digital publishing. These books in collection establish lineages that reiterate the value of the literary magazine: both Kinzie and Anderson (Documentary History) and Morris and Diaz (Contemporary America) refer directly to Hoffman et al., and their ideas are reiterated in WithoutCovers (though without citation) in the words of early online editors Walter Cummins of The Literary Review and  Paula E. Geyh of PostModern American Fiction. 
Take for example Gilbert Sorrentino, editor of NEON, a mimeograph-and-staple litmag of the 1950s (Kinzie & Anderson, 1978, pp. 298-316). Sorrentino modeled NEON after the Black Mountain Institute’s Black Mountain Review and Cid Cormin’s Origin, also featured in Documentary History, but his impetus began with his connection to William Carlos Williams, who told him “a [litmag’s] only rationale is its editor’s belief that the writers he prints must be presented as a group” and Ezra Pound, who connected him to the editors of the Australian magazine Meanjin. Later, his connection to Amiri Baraka, featured in the same collection still as Leroi Jones, resulted in collaborations on Kulchur and Yugen. Sorrentino attributes the success of Kulchur, despite its financial trouble, to the assertion that “The regular reader knew where the editors stood, and hence their criticism made sense; it had roots” (Kinzie & Anderson, 1978, p. 312). In writing the history of specific litmags through the characters that worked behind it and on it, in letting these stories be told in their own voices, we establish that the aura of the litmag extends beyond the text to the folks who were around it, fleshing out the stories behind artifacts. Editorial habitus is much easier to identify in these narratives because they come directly from the writers, enabling the kind of evaluative rubric that Philpotts reaches for in his article. Another benefit of these narratives is that they enable a certain kind of lossy network mapping that I will speak about in the fourth quarter. 
Two limitations persist in this methodology: one is that editors become the spokesperson and the figurehead for magazines that were really collaborations between a much larger set of contributors, readers, publishers, and typesetters. The other limitation is that these biographies and anthologies featured virtually none of the work published in the original magazines. Each of them only has a couple spreads with the cover designs from some of the issues, and the headshots of the editors who contributed, or a group photo that’s a who’s who of the scene at the time. The need to preserve the practical skills necessary to execute one’s own magazine, and the need to preserve the magazines themselves, remains for another quarter to act on. 
Technology implicated in the at-riskness of the literary magazine as an editorial project, via the anthology, when book after book, editors bemoan the emergence of new technology or assume that the version of technology they are using is the final version of acceptable lit mags. Michael Joyce, the hypertext writer, recalls a conversation with cyberliterature theorist Stuart Moulthrop in WithoutCovers: “little magazines were assailed and marginalized by mass-market publishing, while they… were likewise assailed and marginalized by departments of literature.… retreated to a fortress of letter-type aesthetics and finely tuned literary sensibility,” when in reality, “there is real reason for little/literary magazines even now to claim a formative role in the development of new electronic media” (Hurliman & Kunakemakorn, 2002, pp. 33-34). Litmags are small, scrappy things, but editors themselves are limited in what they’re able to make and maintain by the limits of their abilities to work with the mimeograph stencil, with HTML, or with a website builder. These editors outsource technical knowledge and production labor to other cultural workers, but if the focus of the research remains on the editors and their personalities, we risk losing valuable cultural and creative knowledge that book artists have been working to preserve on the side. In doing so, we lose our sense of a litmag as a material artifact. 
Avant-Garde Literature
The avant-garde nature of the literary magazine was already set out and explicitly stated at the start of this paper, and it will be useful to consider (since the margins of creative writing have a circumference much longer than the center) in what various ways a litmag might seek avant-garde writing and publication. This way, we can consider what methods in literary criticism end up moving us towards a better understanding of the litmag in its situation with a couple of examples. If we take the principle that the avant-garde litmag seeks to publish only writing that is not expedient to the mass-market publisher, then we can identify the many things that make writing valuable or illegible to main-stream publishing and propose the axes along which the avant-gardes move. The Little Magazine in Contemporary America (Morris & Diaz, 2015) actually organizes its chapters around these ideas, starting with the most direct, which we’ve already discussed: some editors begin magazines to directly contradict the publishing paradigms of their time. In one essay in their book, Keith Gessen, writing about n+1, writes about how the magazine came into shape as an idea, a prototype, that took literary culture very seriously, even if not itself so (n+1 as a title nods to that it’s another magazine among many, pp. 39-40). 
“Over the years, we published polemics against literary readings, author photos, the publishing process, cell phones, the WSJ, the NYT, Gawker.com, Pitchfork.com, Jewish magazines, right-wing magazines, lit bloggers, video games, the university, AOL chat, the iPod, book reviews, cultural sociology, Harper’s, the Atlantic, the South Asian diaspora, and “world literature” (Morris & Diaz, 2015, p. 46)
Before n+1 became a Whiting Award-winning literary nonprofit, it was a litmag that positioned itself against the mainstream. Its avant-garde position was less about style or experimentation and more about friction with culture that was being taken for granted. Contemporary America’s other units lay out the other axes of the avant-garde for litmags today, which dimly reflect the “binding concepts” in Hoffman et al.: political and activist writing, formal experimentation, the university journal, and litmags on the Web. 
The risk to the avant-garde is that at some point the movement dies. Either it is quelled and repressed, or that which was avant-garde at one point is no longer recognized as transgressive. Take for example WithoutCovers, which deals at length with whether literary magazines should be websites, or even have websites to begin with. The avant-garde-ness of a digital literary magazine at one point was evident in the fact that it was digital at all! Now, digital literary magazines are a dime a dozen. Lori Emerson, opens Reading Writing Interfaces, which will be discussed more in the following essay, with an epigraph by Siegfried Zielinski she describes this way:  “If the twenty-first century does not have… a craving for media, it is because media, by way of interface, are steadily making their way toward invisibility, imperceptibility, and inoperability” (Emerson, 2014, p. 1). Technology is regularly one of the hinges on which the avant-garde swings, but as technologies are normalized and absorbed into mainstream culture, they lose the ability to be avant-garde by definition: the writer must take the technology further than its base use-function. 
Literary criticism is able to do the avant-garde tracing work in ways that archives, network analyses, and editorial biographies simply cannot. A prime example of this is Jessica Pressman’s  Digital Modernism, which unites Marshall McLuhan’s media studies with the literary and media-bound criticism of N. Katherine Hayles to examine media-reflexive, electronic and digital literature (she uses ‘electronic’ and ‘digital’ interchangeably). For Pressman, “Making it new” (the modernist mantra attributed to Ezra Pound, though its history is older than him) “is a recursive act of engaging with a literary past through media”— a “rearview mirror” that anyone making literature, even print literature, nowadays looks into to make new creative writing and media artifacts. Literary criticism like Pressman’s fills in the gaps and strings together 100 years of litmag history to make sense of how the legacy avant-gardes and new media avant-gardes are really in conversation with each other, and how our explorations today are dependent on past forms to take shape in the digital. 
While literary criticism both immediately after a work’s publication and in retrospective are crucial in helping us make comprehensible the movements of avant-garde writing toward the new or against the old, without preservation of the artifacts themselves, both old and new, we risk only having stories of stories, lore handed down about texts that no longer exist. On one hand, this makes criticism indispensable: because of the loss of many media formats, all that survives of many avant-garde texts is the discussion of them written at the time that they were still accessible. Maybe the files still exist (see the NEXT), but the technologies used to read encoded files, such as Flash, and the awareness of how old media production formats that would explain the visual and tactile dimensions of a format, get lost without good literary criticism and close readings of avant-garde writing, and media preservation, which I will discuss in the next section. Pressman’s Digital Modernisms defines “reading machines” as a devise that stores and presents literature, and says that these too are open to literary critique (Pressman, 2014, p. 57).
Time Capsules / Special Collections
            The next major methodology I want to discuss is the digitization and archivization movement in literary magazines, which treats the archival process as a project and study of magazines, as well as treating the magazines themselves as an archive. Hoffman et al. speak about Margaret Anderson’s Little Review in this way: 
The new symbolists were making their first appearances, and Miss Anderson found herself fighting tooth and nail for the right of America to read [them]. Also at this time [1916] she began her fight for the privilege of introducing to America such literary movements as cubism, futurism, and expressionism. (1947, p. 57)
They then use Anderson’s story, and the trajectory of The Little Review to explain the emergence of dada and surrealism in the United States, saying “it was one of the few outlets in this country for ideas and techniques which were to influence profoundly much of our later writing” (p. 60). The Little Review, in their imagination, is a time capsule for the experimentalist movements of the day. The litmag is an archive, and a special collection of magazines is an archive of archives. In his article “Archival Dispersals: Literary Magazines as Mobile and Fragmentary Archives,” Mert Bahadir Reisoglu puts a finer point on this. “As an alternative type of archive,” he notes that Turkish-German litmags saw themselves as archives of Turkish life in Germany and of literature “to meet the cultural needs of Turks in Germany and to recover the legacy of forgotten authors” (Reisoglu, 2022, p. 54). Riesoglu compares this to the nationalist archive, which purports to be abundant and whole, representative of the nation, and his work makes way for the sense that a litmag can archive that which the nation will not, and that the litmag will collect the edges trimmed off, the fragments of literary culture that get left out because they are “unrul[y].” Near the end of his article, he says, “as a radical dispersion, the magazine always faces the risk of becoming invisible,” a statement about the at-riskness of the litmag as an archive (p. 58). What if the artifact dies? What if it is burned instead of preserved? What happens when the domain, the index, the site, or the library, expires? 
            From this perspective, it is no surprise that librarians and periodical scholars have spent countless hours and many grant-funded projects recording, preserving digitizing, and tagging litmags into searchable archives. The last third of The Little Magazine is a full bibliography of little magazines, a task taken up later not just by other researchers of literary magazines, by librarians and indexers, but by the magazines themselves, which often publish reading lists of similar or companion magazines (Fernández, 2024; Kinzie & Anderson, 1978; Ludovico & Montfort, 2023). But even the archives of litmags are precarious. In a Journal of Modern Periodical Studies special issue on the Archive, published in 2017, Clifford Wulfman describes the Blue Mountain and Modernist Journals Projects. Both were grant-funded archival projects that will need to find new foundations to continue. Wulfman warns that digital libraries should not be confused with digitized libraries, and stresses that “a new alliance of scholars, technologists, and publishers,” is needed to create digital “critical editions of historical magazines” rather than just digital facsimiles or scans of old publications (Wulfman, 2017). But Eric Bulson, in the same issue, traces back the many different ways that litmags have been archived (microfilm, reprints, amateur photocopy, etc.), and speaks more hopefully about the many options we have available to preserve litmags. 
Last, how do we decide what to archive, and of what use is it? Harriet Green argues that literary magazines should be archived and collected by libraries because faculty are publishing scholarship in creative and online journals, arguing that “literary journals and magazines… have largely been absent in the dominant analyses for disciplinary scholarship” (Wulfman, 2017). In her study, she codes all of the poems, stories, and articles written across 19 magazines, many of which are print but two of which are online, and identifies that between 10 and 60 percent of publications across those magazines come from academically-affiliated authors. Most often, these publications are articles and book reviews, but poetry and fiction appear as well in the set, but Green argues that libraries need to better support the academics publishing in litmags as a result of this. Identifying that literature networks writers at universities with litmags and reviews, Green employs the last analysis method I will discuss to justify the archival methodologies I have just discussed. 
Social Networks 
            In discussing the Double Dealer, a southern magazine of the early 1920s, Hoffman et al. note that while the magazine published a lot of “second-rate poetry and stories,” “they clearly recognized a most important function of the little magazine—that of encouraging unknown writers” (p. 13). The Double Dealer was the first to publish both Hemingway and Faulkner; Hoffman et al. reference the magazines as communities where writers introduce themselves and begin to correspond with other editors and writers and share their work more widely. In being able to recognize the litmag publishing scenes as social networks, we begin to be able to map out magazines and the connections they represent between aesthetics, movements, political figures, international writers, and so on. To Alexandra Reza, the connections between the anticolonial magazines, transcontinental in their locations and publications, are as important as the shape that the writing took in the magazines: “I bring a concern with coalitions across imperial boundaries, and with form” (Reza, 2024, p. 11). Reza’s book investigates how the anticolonial journals, composed of emergent and established voices in different languages, questioned the colonial states they traversed, not by fusing all the voices into a singular consensus, but by moving back and forth between questions of identity, space and place, and different political imaginaries. Reza doesn’t focus on a singular journal or editor because to do so wouldn’t be able to capture trends in the anticolonial literary movements in any applicable way. 
            In the digital age, the networks between writers are not just metaphorical, but literal— the connections between literary magazines are hyperlinked, dynamic and literal. WithoutCovers describes the excitement of literary editors to jump on this technological capacity: Marion Wrenn of Painted Bride Quarterly: writes “Going online helped us see that Painted Bride Quarterly is less engaged in the commercial economy and much more so in a “gift economy”…. By remaining in print, and by keeping our print runs painfully small, our “gifts” went ungiven” (Hurliman & Kunakemakorn, 2002, p. 95). Digital network mapping has also emerged as a major methodology for investigating the literary magazine, as a JMPS special issue on network analysis displays. 
In my next essay, I’ll take the at-riskness of the digital networking of litmags at greater length, because what really is at stake is what happens to a litmag when its platform is rug-pulled (“rug pull” as a verb comes from NFT scamming: developers hype a project, drive up the price, then dump it, leaving investors with worthless assets). The excitement of avant-garde poetics pushes writers, editors, and poets into new technological spaces and interests, but when a web domain or host goes out of business, a platform is discontinued, and so on, we lose the litmag, too. The NEXT archives electronic literature litmags and publications that were built on “obsolete” platforms, and as early as 2015, Rebecca Morgan Frank in The Little Magazine in Contemporary America speculated that the Wayback Machine would remain of huge importance in preserving digitally networked magazines without print counterparts (such as can we have our ball back?, an early 2000s digital litmag; Morris & Diaz, 2015, p. 200). 
The network maps that researchers build themselves help us discover connections between writers, editors, and litmags that weren’t explicitly obvious. In one study in JMPS, Jana Smith Elford uses network analysis’s visual and computational powers to bring previously marginalized stories to light in the study of Fabian News. The distant reading of five years of the newsletter helped Elford discover a network of proto-feminist writing that had been left out of earlier studies, and back up those networks with statistical measures like degree centrality and betweenness. Network analysis can help us reevaluate our previous narratives about litmags, and corroborate or challenge the narratives about them that we receive from their editors in the first-pass of history. Network analysis also helps us consider the whole scale of a litmag at once, given clean data—editors live their magazines issue by issue—Elford stresses that network analysis can help us begin to recover patterns and “hypothesize,” meaning that work must still be handed off to experts to theorize under further investigation and closer reading. But this way of looking at the litmag is possible because of two major paradigms, one that sees litmags as collections of individuals, and one interested in reading litmags through the machine. In conclusion, this makes network analysis itself a precarious methodology, like archivism— if the funding runs out for the work or the platform one uses to network-map goes under or can’t be run on the latest computers, we risk losing a powerful way of seeing in the future.  
Conclusion 
In this essay, I argue that different ways of researching litmags emerged from the different ways we’ve characterized the litmag to begin with. Litmags are precarious at each of these main points; that is, editors, the avant-garde, the artifacts, and social networks are all precarious because of the limits of our relationship to emerging technologies. As someone who’s studied the avant-garde feminist poetry in Ms. magazine, rendered visible the social network inherent to the contributors’ notes in DIAGRAM, runs a digital litmag that would crash if I just didn’t pay the domain fee anymore, and is hoping to interview editors directly about their tension with the Generative AI landscape currently, each of these concerns is important to me, but only tells one part of the story at a time. Concurrently, though, these ways of investigating the litmag are intertwined. I couldn’t have studied the poetry in Ms. without the bound collection of magazines at Alabama, or the digitized covers on the Ms. website. I wouldn’t have used DIAGRAM for my network analysis if Ander Monson, its editor, hadn’t gone to my same MFA program. Each of these projects and methodologies—the anthology, the archive, the network, and the literary theory—will do its part against the death of litmags, and we need each of them to hold onto as much of the story of litmags as possible. 

Platforms and Cultural Production opens with the “Adpocalypse”, which is the day headlines broke that ad revenues from major brands were funding, unknown to those companies, the social media channels of hate organizations, extremist religious groups, and so on (Poell et al., 2022). As headline after headline broke, media hosts like YouTube revised their Terms of Service to protect advertisers from appearing next to controversial content: they adjusted content moderation algorithms, implemented categorical rules, and mischaracterized content. LGBTQ creators were flagged as sexually aggressive, and Assassin’s Creed walkthrough and gameplay videos were demonetized because of the term “assassin,” and broad swaths of non-controversial content was disrupted in an effort to govern where ad revenue would be allocated (pp. 1-3).  This opening example, which tees up the premise of Platforms and Cultural Production that platformization as a concept is best expressed as the intertwining of digital platforms and data infrastructures (technology, money, and governance) with cultural work, creative expression, and life together. In literary publishing, there was recently a similar revelation: an “AIpocalypse,” the day that science fiction literary magazine Clarkesworld closed their submission portal to mitigate the flood of algorithmically-generated sci-fi (Clarke, 2023). Like in the Adpocalypse, authentic human creators were denied access to publish in a paying literary market because of algorithmic implementations. Whether it’s a protective algorithm or a productive one, new technologies like “AI” disrupt the existing cultural ecosystem and raise the alarm among users: What will we do? Who do we have to fight? This reality is not new for long-time publishers—social media, computers, and copymachines had their own impacts on literary publishing, for better or for worse. We cannot discount how precedented these times are, but it does not change that they are realities editors and writers must contend with.

4.     Maintaining Platformization as a purely digital concept might allow us to ignore how certain legacy media, like magazines, precede the digital as multi-user markets where readers, writers, editors, and advertisers interface on matters of culture;
Before proceeding, I would like to issue a caveat. It is a reflex of my attunement as a writer to often over-apply or over-compare two things that are truly very distinct from one another. I hope that in the pages to come my comparison of profoundly different media phenomena will not be taken as trying to make broad equivalences between the little magazine in the 1900s, for example, and the platform economies of 2010s and 2020s. Instead, as I interrogate the political economy of the platformed litmag moving forward, I think it’s useful to look backward at preceding eras and notice the ways that earlier litmags were bound to the political economies of the small press, and to consider what parallels can be drawn so that we can trace the shadows of the past in the present. I will use differences between contemporary digital litmags and traditional, print litmags, to flesh out these points. 
Interlude: What Kinds of Platforms Are There? 
While Platformization has a sense for the ways that platforms and data infrastructures intertwine with cultural work, Poell et al. provide a specific definition of the platform that it will be important to delineate before moving forward. It’s here that I want to provide a framework for distinguishing between different kinds of platforms, which will also help us see the similarities between legacy media and new media. To Poell, Nieborg, and Duffy, platforms are “data infrastructures that facilitate, aggregate, monetize, and govern interactions between end-users and content and service providers.” To put this another way, platforms are encoding/decoding communication technologies with a range of interacting stakeholders. In a moment, I map out various stakeholders and whether they can communicate and interact with one another or not, to make a point about the range of technologies that get called “platforms.” First, I want to trace a brief story about platforms that show us that platforms are themselves platformed, which perpetuates the problems, excitements, risks, and opportunities of new media. I position this as a story, or as a series of stages, but as Table 2 below shows, I want to illuminate the number of operations that are absorbed into our use of a platform, that have been normalized, so that we can distinguish what kinds of literary platforms exist today. 
            The platforms for literary production have been in flux since the internet came to be. An older definition of platforms positions platforms as computational hardware that producers wrote games and other media for. In the vein of almost all the texts in MIT Press’s Platform Studies series, “platform” refers to the Atari, GameBoy, Commodore, Super NES, Wii, Kindle, and so on, to “consoles.” In a literary sense, the platform defined this way seems like it would only align with interactive narratives one might plug in to one of these consoles and run, apart from which the story experience is inaccessible. 
From the perspective of electronic literature practitioners, platformization simply gives language to one of the risks inherent in designing digital writing projects contingent on a certain software or hardware encoding the project to be read. This is the premise of Matthew Kirschenbaum’s book Bitstreams: The Future of Digital Literary Heritage, which begins with (who else?) Ezra Pound and T.S. Eliot. It took almost a hundred years to index in chronological order the paper versions of Eliot’s The Waste Land by comparing the typewriters and paper he worked on over several years, but what if it had been composed on a computer? Would this archaeology—this bibliography—be possible? Kirschenbaum’s bitstream, the way that all digital production converges into the ones and zeroes of digital communication, does not mean that digital production is arbitrary, but rather, since it is material amounts of electricity somewhere, it’s as contingent as the paper copies that could be lost in an archive or burned in a house fire (Kirschenbaum, 2021).  
Besides the precarity of digital hardware’s obsolescence, software platforms like Flash come into play, approaching something more like the sense of the word “Platform” that Poell et al. gesture at. Adobe Flash and Hypercard are software platforms, not hardware platforms, and they were used both to read and to write interactive fictions and poetry. The Eastgate Quarterly Review of Hypertext was perhaps the first litmags platformed litmag in this older sense, and the fact that these works were labor-intensive to develop and are extremely difficult to come by now (some are fifty dollars, arrive as a USB, and still require HyperCard to run) point at the limitations of early platforms to sustain literary dialogue. The limitations of Flash as a platform for electronic literature became apparent when the platform was discontinued, immediately throwing hundreds, if not thousands, of works into obsolescence. In Bitstreams, Kirschenbaum describes his project of uncovering the version history, files, and actual disks of William Dickey’s Hypercard poems from Deena Larsen, who still had Dickey’s Macintosh and had worked to maintain and repair some of Dickey’s Hyperpoems. Since then, there have been emulators created so that one can view them, “But the bitstream is never truly self-identical,” Kirschenbaum says (pp. 50-55); a work’s appearance in remediated formats changes its meaning.
Then there are platforms that write software or interactive media that are not required to read their products—they are packaged in such a way that once they are exported, the object is free-floating. Either the artifact requires a different platform to be read (a general computer or another software), or the artifact is materialized. Twine is one example: one writes interactive narrative in Twine, which is exported as an HTML file that can be read by anyone’s browser, or re-platformed and hosted on a digital platform like GitHub or one’s personal domain. Adobe InDesign is a platform that writes printable media from PDFs to image files, which could either be opened on a computer or materialized directly. 
The next stage of platformization is the removal of the platforms from individual software downloaded to one’s computer to the Internet, which is housed in external servers and in the cloud, remotely accessible from anywhere via an Internet connection. Whether a digital platform is writing and storing files (such as WordPress or Substack) or is a social communications platform (such as Meta’s Facebook and Instagram), platforms here are their own domains, territories that are governed and moderated and interacted with by a variety of parties. Many of these platforms rely on both content creation and communications, either to form datasets to run AI on or to make Ad Revenue, and so on. Not all platforms are created equal though, and these distinctions become really important in the literary context: Poetree, a poem-social media, is not the same as ChillSubs, or Submittable, or the LitMagNews Substack, or the re•mediate website.

TABLE 2: Digital Platforms Perform a Range of Interactive Functions.
The sheer amount of communication between roles on a digital platform necessitates Poell et al.’s wide-scoped definition of platforms as “data infrastructures” that handle communication between a variety of actors in the digital space. Whether a platform is more text or video forward may not make much a difference in how platform-y it is if one is comparing a social media platform with a full online store to a blog. Users “read” Netflix, but don’t “write” on that site the way they do on YouTube. The point of drawing this out is that in the print world, a poetry collection is like a single Netflix show with ordered content all by the same author, while,  comparatively, litmags are YouTube, wherein advertisers, users, artists, writers, editors, and designers all have a unique and visible fingerprint on the experience. When litmags are remediated digitally, or even are born-digital, there are more actors with thumbprints on the scales than just users: platforms and bots are interacting in subtle and at times overt ways. 
1.     Content in digital publishing is bound by extra markets and infrastructures. 
When you picked up a copy of a litmag off the newsstand in 1950, you knew what you were paying for. Maybe the magazine paid contributors (you could find this in the submissions guidelines); they might have hired a printer, and then the material cost of the ink and paper was in your own hands. If you picked up a zine in the 90s, there may have been even less exchange: maybe the xerox machine was used clandestinely, and you found the zine for free on a little rack in a club or at a zine-sharing event. There is little use romanticizing this way of publishing, which still exists, for its ostensible cheapness, but I start here because platforms too portray themselves as similarly cheap, even democratic. It’s “free” to be on Facebook, and “free” to start a Substack, and “free” to start a WordPress blog (with asterisks). But the markets binding twentieth-century print magazines were the markets for literature, postage, paper, ink, printing, and distribution. As they are platformed, litmags participate in some of the same, some separate production markets from the print journal. Some of these are even hidden from the editors, who are alienated from their participation in the cloud computing markets that WordPress, ReadyMag, or Amazon are directly selecting, building, etc. So while a digital litmag editor is still participating in the market for writers, and some in printing and distribution, they’ve left the markets of postage, paper, and ink behind for the markets of the submission portal, hosting software and site builders, and social media promotion. As the editor of a journal with two social media accounts (Bluesky and Instagram), I realize that I’m at the mercy of the algorithm for exposure to my content, so I budget a small amount of money to pay Instagram (from my computer, to avoid Apple’s app store fees) to promote my submission call a month before it closes.
2.     Content in digital publishing is bound by interfaces and platform governance, not just by gatekeepers and their tastes.
The infrastructures of digital publishing have changed how readers interface with literary products, which is a profoundly useful paradigm to return to print with as well. In Reading Writing Interfaces, Lori Emerson builds off of Floridan Cramer’s 8 different kinds of interfaces (which is reminiscent of the Table 2, above, that I constructed on a separate occasion during Dr. Stanfill’s social media class in Spring 2025). Emerson opens up her definition of interface to any technology “that mediates between reader and the surface-level, human-authored writing, as well as… machine-based writing taking place below the gloss of the surface. The interface is, then, a threshold” (Emerson, 2014, p. x). In the book, Emerson works backwards from the interfaces that are most designed to not look like interfaces at all, such as multitouch devices like the iPad, to the first Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs), to typewriter concrete poetry, to Emily Dickinson’s use of extra pieces of paper that would pin up and change the ending to poems she wrote (Emerson, 2014). In each chapter, Emerson describes the kinds of media-reflexive poetics at the heart of Jessica Pressman’s investigations of McLuhan’s digital modernist poetics (“form and content are inseparable”; Pressman, 2014, p. 45). Emerson concludes by celebrating contemporary media artists and media writing, especially that which critiques the monopoly Google has over most corners of the internet, especially the Terms of Service consolidation of 2012, which binds, therefore, most literary producers using digital services and products. “Thus, our agreement to these TOS amounts to our consenting to passive consumption of whatever information Google chooses to present to us in whatever form it deems appropriate” (p. 170). This is a literary critic with a sense for the platformization of digital publishing, with the same senses for the materiality of the internet as Kirschenbaum. 
To circle back around, there are several applications of Emerson’s way of reading these interfaces for digital publishers, especially the quotation above. Some older blog and HTML litmags try to make ad revenue from Google AdSense, but the kinds of ads that Google serves these small sites ends up seriously disrupting the experience of the work. On another hand, editors’ inability to interface skillfully with digital platforms result in not being able to solicit any work that doesn’t arrive in a left-aligned, 12-pt. font Word document ready to be copied and pasted into the “Post” feature of WordPress. On the other hand, litmags like Tilted House’s project TILT specifically reject any poems that could be presented this way (Goldkamp, n.d.). The media-reflexive digital litmag invites its own challengers. 
When one adds an extra layer of platformization, especially when a litmag has a social media presence, there are even more ways that the governance infrastructures like Terms of Service documents and algorithmic content moderation software also provide distinct challenges to litmags. As the Overton window shifts and social media platforms cater to children (as well as through the spread of algorithmic folklore about what will be censored or demonetized on any given platform), litmags, especially those that publish avant garde, politically leftist, and transgressive content in avant garde forms, will face repercussions, shadowbanning, deactivations, and so on. Platformization offers clarity of sight so that editors and writers know what to expect from platforms, what the limits to their rights are, and where they can resist. 
3.     Focusing on platformization risks reducing creative writing to language and text.  
In Reading Writing Interfaces, Lori Emerson quotes Jason Nelson: “Within many digital poems there is one commonality, the emphasis on interface… These interfaces are not just vessels for content, they are poems in themselves” (Emerson, 2014, p. 40). While platformization helps us see how cultural producers are (either to a small or large degree) platform dependent, it doesn’t teach us how to respond. Tactical publishing “can reclaim a humanistic approach,” he says in his introduction,  which is the kind of humanities-based, artistic resistance to platformization as a phenomenon that we need more of (Ludovico & Montfort, 2023). Ludovico has been resisting the high promises of digitization and digital publishing as ‘the’ way forward since his first book Post-Digital Print, in which he pointed to the magazine as a network node (Ludovico, 2012). In Tactical Publishing, he details the artistic, transgressive modes of publishing that, in media-reflexive ways, challenge the given-ness of any media paradigm, such as printing a book with ink mixed with HIV+ blood that is totally safe to read, or all of the non-human creative writing bots on Twitter. As Danny Snelson does in The Little Database (2025), Ludovico implores us to eschew the digital publishing paradigms of endlessness and infinity and to create bounded, curated libraries instead, to participate in a healthier media ecology. 
4.     If platformization is just digital, we risk divorcing the story of new- from legacy media
Last, the platformization of litmags doesn’t necessarily have to change their vision or mission. In a study conducted in response to Hurliman and Kunakemakorn’s WithoutCovers, Paling and Nilan find that online magazines felt even more strongly about some of the values of the litmag as they are traditionally defined by Hoffman et al. and Bourdieu. They conducted interviews of more than 30 editors of digital litmags, and nearly all of their coded transcripts mapped onto the four classic values of the litmag: high positive regard for autonomy, for the avant-garde, and for symbolic capital (prestige), and a disregard of financial profit. Print magazines, in their study, also had a high regard for autonomy and prestige, but were more concerned about financial viability and less concerned on average about avant-gardism (Paling & Nilan, 2006). While the processes of publishing have become digital, not all forms are digital publishing per se. Matthew Kirschenbaum discusses in Bitstreams how the method of digital print layout via Adobe InDesign has moved the publication and design of books closer to something like “commercial art direction” (2021, p. 76). Digital methods have replaced analog methods for laying out books, for example, but these methods still result in a traditional print book. As Ludovico writes, “we should use these systems, and other industrial systems we already use, to inspire us. More than ever, we have the chance to discover” (Ludovico & Montfort, 2023, p. 218). Digital publishing can be discerning in using the platforms we’ve been provided and courageous in subverting them with media-reflexive art.
Conclusion
Overall, I find that platformization will be a helpful tool for scholars and editors interested in the critical dimensions and implications of their work. Litmag publishers are, as I quoted in the previous essay, assailed, and now not just by the digital in general or by the mass market publishing engine, but also by AI. With the emergence of the Frankenstein’s monster that AI represents to many in publishing (print and digital alike, now), it is more and more difficult not to reflect on the wholesale adoption of digital apparatuses and not recognize the ways that publishers might have been playing directly into the hands of companies that could steal all the books we digitized and the entire breadth of the internet we preserved and made open-access to train large language models. Platformization is the critical modality through which we can appropriately critique the explosion of artificially-generated text, edit discerningly, and make better-informed choices about the ways we publish from now on. Digital publishers benefit from platformization because it presumes that the ways that we platform, the places we initiate a public digital presence, will be bound up in sets of trade-offs, whether they be financial, ethical, climate, political, or privacy-based. Platformization also gives us the space to separate our  professional motives from the passion we have for the art that we make, for the communities we really want to make, and to choose spaces that benefit us more and megacorporations less.
 
 

 
In creative culture in the 21st Century, several digital practices have become key, rather than optional or peripheral, aspects of the labor of someone in a creative career. As the question precipitating this essay notes, digital creators are taking on self-promotional work to maintain their careers. Self-promotion takes a variety of forms, and each one that I’ve chosen to write about here remediates the protocols of literary behavior by writers and publishers into promotional material. In media studies, “protocols” refer to the “designed core architecture” of a genre; they’re the frameworks for how we think about, organize, read, and interpret literature (Kilbane, 2025, pp. 6-14). The tasks of this essay are (1) to locate the protocols of platformed self-promotion in the literature about social media labor and on the field of cultural production and (2) to show how these several modes of promotional material—the Branded Self, the Behind the Scenes, the Event, and the Artifact —remediate the protocols of Authorship, Criticism, and Publishing into digitally-marketable artifacts: into content about ‘being an Author,’ ‘being a Reader,’ and ‘being a Publisher.’ I remain, as the authors and writers I will write about here are, in a critically ambivalent space. On one hand, I will draw on platformization, which sees online expression as bounded by technological and software infrastructures that contextualize our perception of otherwise normal behavior; on the other, I will rely on lines of thought in critical media studies, which makes meanings of and critiques how creators play into platform cultures. 
Content. 
Content, as Kate Eichhorn lays out in her book of the same name, takes on several activities in the current media paradigm. On one hand, content is a social currency by which creators purchase relevance, circumventing the need for cultural capital, which is earned and paid for by access to institutions, cultural events, and one’s own skills, expertise, and activity. Both cultural and content capital are the proof that affords someone position-taking power, to call themselves, for example, an Author (Eichhorn, 2022, p. 84-5). On the other hand, from a platformization perspective, content is the raw material which affords platforms a draw for users, which affords the sale of users to advertisers, and which affords well-trained algorithms, and which affords the sale of priority placement back to creators. In my last essay, I centered platformization as a way to attend to how digital/data infrastructures intertwine with culture and creativity and convert communities and aesthetic expressions into governed marketplaces of digital artifacts. In this essay, because platforms are the stage on which these relationships and exchanges between creators and audiences take place, platformization will be one mode of criticism that helps us view the current paradigm in creative career-making. At the same time that authors and writers acquire cultural capital by attending schools, workshops, and publish in prestigious litmags, they are also expected to maintain a public persona on one or more platforms. On these platforms, they post content, not the PDF files of or links to their books—they publish representations of the artifacts they create in a stream of texts designed to frame and brand the creative work. 
Influencer Creep. 
Sophie Bishop identifies the transformation of the work identity into content creation as “influencer creep”, and identifies three cultural practices as the tenets of her book Influencer Creep: “Optimization, Authenticity, and Self-Branding” [1] (Bishop, 2025b). These practices pervade the practices of authorial self-promotion online. Kate Eichhorn’s book Content provides a way to cross-section out different kinds of content: content marketing, publishing content, educational content, and entertainment content (Eichhorn, 2022, pp. 22-26), which I believe map onto the modes of content authors make (readings, merch, original works, and confessionals). In the media economy, authors don’t just write the ‘text’ but all sorts of paratexts to set readers’ expectations and agendas and prime them for engagement and participation. These ideas have been simmering in literary, platform, and media studies, which especially in the case of literary celebrity and magazine publishing seem inextricable from one another in many ways. However, scholars from different epistemological backgrounds and academic disciplines provide different insights into the effects of the phenomenon of digital presence on our experiences of literature. 
As I make examples of individual cases, I will be referring to them as capital-letter Writers and Publishers, because while each human participating in the digital economy has a subjective, lived experience, they exist online through a self-positioning as public figures. The authenticity Writers communicate is ambivalent— it may be an expression of real intentions and realities, but it’s curated for the brand. Brooke Erin Duffy treats this phenomenon at length in (Not) Getting Paid to Do What You Love, which treats the emergence of the content creator’s work as forward-looking labor—“aspirational laborers expect that they will one day be compensated for their productivity—be it through material rewards or social capital” (Duffy, 2022, pp. 5-6, emphasis Duffy's). Duffy’s book orbits several contradictions inherent to this kind of work: “Authenticity vs. Self-promotion,” “Creativity vs. Commerce,” and “Hobby vs. Professional Status” (pp. 6-7). Sarah Banet-Weiser identified these paradoxes as “ambivalences” (2012), language Sophie Bishop cites in Influencer Creep, and Duffy identifies the desire for “authenticity” as a unique feature of self-promotion by aspirational workers in the media space compared to legacy media, which maintained the distance between stars and audiences (now, the remediation of new media paradigms into legacy media is starting to blur this distinction).
Precedent. 
This paradigm for what self-promotional artistic labor looks like is based on previous articulations of remediated literary expression, as Mike Chasar shows in Poetry Unbound. Poetry’s easy mobility is its “chief, most distinctive, and most important features,” Chasar says (p. 4), and he traces poetry’s ability to be remediated from magic lantern slides of the early 20th century through film intertitles and features in movies and television to Instapoetry in the early 21st. In “From Murder to Milk and Honey,” Chasar positions the litmag (the modernist projects of individual editors to collect and publish quality avant-garde writing to niche audiences) as a transformative medium that put a personality editor between the writer and their readership. In the system of cultural capital, “little magazine editors were increasingly charged with the task of ‘differentiating experimentation from nonsense’…. and ‘changed poets’ relation to their own voices,’” quoting Elizabeth Barnett’s essay “Destroyed by Poetry: Alice Corbin and the Little Magazine Effect” (Chasar, 2020, p. 175-176). I want to highlight the similarity between publishing in litmags as Chasar describes and ‘publishing’ on social media platforms, because algorithmic content distribution similarly forces authors to contend with their relationship to their own voice. Even if writers and authors purportedly may define what it means to be a writer by themselves and create content that frames being a poet through personal expression, there’s still an algorithm between the content and its received audience. Media is transmissible, but the poet’s personality, process, performances, and products take shape as platformed content so that they can train the algorithm and the audience to receive the work in the desired way. When they speak to one another about online self-promotion, creators contribute to the circulation of algorithmic folklore, the rumors and shared knowledge that communities come up with to explain the success and failure of content in the algorithmically-mediated environment. 
            Why is there a hunger for content in the first place, though? Did artists come up with this themselves? Jessica Pressman identifies this hunger for digital artifacts about books as  “bookishness,” and traces three outcomes of bookish culture that are relevant to this essay. One, bookishness renegotiates of the symbolism of the book from “privacy, leisure, individualism, knowledge, and power” towards “proximity, interiority, and authenticity” in the digital era. The book becomes an icon that symbolizes class, personal ties, beliefs, and affiliations. Two, bookishness reifies the materiality of the book as an object—of affection, as decoration, as a souvenir, as a container for memory. Third, in her conclusion, books transcend the cliché that books let you “travel without moving an inch,” (Lahiri, 2019, generally misquoted as "Books let you travel without moving your feet"). Not only do the stories in books take place in new places, but books are graphic visual media we carry around to signal our bookishness to others around us in public. 
The situation of the literary worker in the platform economy is one marked by ambivalence, a term which is used in feminist media scholarship to identify the multiple lenses through which creative production online might be seen, and the both/and nature of the contradictions like those Duffy defined above. Rupi Kaur, the most-followed Instapoet (a type of influencer and celebrity who earned content capital by creating poetry content), posts poems that are authentic expressions, but they are for sure also “content marketing,” which Eichhorn defines as “valuable and relevant” content that builds an audience ((Eichhorn, 2022, p. 22). The photos of Kaur with her poems spread out in pages on the floor are evidence of process, but they also present Kaur as an author with a work-in-progress, and attach her to the materiality of writing, as well as to Instagram. 
Images of Kaur writing at her desk or on vacation draw the reader past the page into the life of the author and maintain a visual and affective relationship between Kaur and her readers for the time between releases of her books and the tours that she takes. I do want to separate the periodicity of an Instagram page from the periodicity of the literary magazine, though. An Instagram page can be a regular publication, a daily or a weekly that features posts and puts them all together into a feed onto a profile, but the way those posts show up among an algorithmically curated feed is not the same as the way those texts are curated together in the literary magazine. A magazine is a curated anthology, a single object that stands alone, and an Instagram feed is an archive of posts that are first experienced as individual artifacts in an algorithmically curated feed. Even if my magazine were to post all nine pieces from the new issue on Instagram one after another, the specific platform affordances of Instagram transform the “magazine” into a different media object than the static page[s] my magazine lives on. Bookishness sometimes conceals the difference between remediations of literary artifacts, though, and divorces the book from its meanings, from its author, and from its book-ness.  
I find evidence of this divorce in the cultural frustration with the “performative reader” type, the person who carries a book and reads it in public as a virtue signal. Bookishness underlies and activates the affective power of the “performative reader” type, but bookishness ignores the ambivalence inherent in “reading in public” (both when it’s seen in the real world and photographed and posted online as content). We risk perceiving the public reader as a communicating to an audience instead of as having an individual experience with a text we share a familiarity with. “Bookishness reminds us that the literary is not only about stories, formal aesthetics, and the love of books but always also about human connection,” (Pressman, 2020, p. 150). When authors generate a (a) branded self, (b) sell merch, (c) hold events, and (d) describe their own work to camera, they do so to connect with their audiences in both real ways and in ways that can become content that effectively primes users to engage and an algorithm to spread their work. 
Caveats. 
Before I dig into the types of self-promotion that are most common to literary work today, I want to mark off the reality that while influencer creep is occurring in literary environments, there are still many more authors, critics, and publishers who are not using the internet at all, or are using it badly. The kinds of events, personalities, artifacts, and merchandise that authors spend time creating could be facilitated by a publisher or expressions of a self-publishing creative; these are even different from what a team at a literary journal can put together. This is to say that in future research, I might draw out a similar graph to Bourdieu’s field of cultural production that maps out the range of digital literary activity and branding to include Instapoets, publishers, book designers, BookTubers and literary criticism influencers, and little magazines and university journals, alongside literary authors who are promoting their works in different ways, because the content that each of these literary stakeholders create to self-promote are not equal or the same. 
Today, Publishers- and Authors-as-Creators position themselves as both niche for advertisers and palatable to the platform, as both unique to their fans and edgy to outsiders, as culturally transgressive and as compliant with the platform norms. Especially in the literary economy, these happen to different degrees of success. Authors and publishers and writers exist on a spectrum of proficiency and awareness and care for the production of content. At the same time, some publishers go well-beyond author-generated content and specifically leverage the book industry, social media, and influencers to promote books, book sales, book releases, book launches, and book awards, literally “booking” fans who have become creators and influencers in their own right on YouTube and Instagram for the purposes of marketing and sales. Jack Ben Edwards is a good example of a book fan, an English major in the UK, who turned his love for books into a full-time influencing career than in the past year and a half has landed him interviews with the stars of several major book adaptations, like Ryan Gosling (Hail Mary), Margot Robbie and Jacob Elordi (Wuthering Heights), and Paul Mescal and Jessie Buckley (Hamnet). Edwards’ content is entertainment; his recommendations of books are educational and content marketing; and the quality, character, and affective experience of the book is the benefit that a BookTuber or Bookfluencer can specifically bring to a publisher’s marketing strategy.
The dark reality, though, is that this kind of work is an expression of the de/recomposition of a labor politic that turns workers into a variety of exp0loited figures, according to Greig de Peuter in “Creative Economy and Labor Precarity” (2011). To turn the Artist, the Editor, the Publisher into the Content Creator is, at best, to make the most of a dismal neoliberal situation that is content to situate workers in a condition of “existential, financial, and social insecurity” (De Peuter, 2011, p. 419). Duffy identifies these creators as doing aspirational labor, what has been previously labeled “hope labor,” free work now tied to the hope of paid work later (Kuehn & Corrigan, 2013). Content capital is what the content creator can accumulate (in the form of likes, followers, and engagement) to justify themselves as useful to larger brands, falling into the labor position that de Peuter calls the “precog”—the driven, self-responsible cognitive worker who “compromis[es] optimistic accounts of the creative class,” and “might have a prestigious occupation but labors under classic precarious conditions” (De Peuter, 2011, p. 420). Creators now bring their content capital to traditional publishers and to the algorithm as proof of concept and as collateral for the risk of publishing their book, similar to celebrities who publish their own poetry collections and bring their fanbases with them as collateral to the publisher (a guaranteed sale).
The Branded Self – Personality as Content 
In the interviews Bishop did for Influencer Creep, ambivalences appear along identity lines like race and gender: “The affordances of Instagram bake mandates toward authenticity into creative culture—influencer creep demands a deeper inclusion of the personal into promotion” (Bishop, 2025a, p. 2122). The mothers and women of color in her study reported that algorithms and audiences on social media rewarded them with better engagement when behind the scenes content included their personal and family lives. Who are users self-promoting their faces to?  It’s multiple audiences or markets: in a platform economy, creators, whether they are writers or influencers for other commodities then creative and aesthetic texts, are marketing themselves with certain expertise and attractiveness to advertisers, brands, and to the platform itself. They don’t want to have their audiences removed from them by the algorithm: 
They needed to include images of their face in their Instagram content, or document their highly emotional moments for two reasons. First, because it was what their audience wanted to see, but second, they were what they perceived the Instagram algorithm wanted them to do. (Bishop, 2025a, p. 2122)
In Reading #instapoetry, Melissa Sarikaya talks about quote “How to be a successful #instapoet” and discusses Holly McNish's marketing strategies, arguing that McNish specifically has a “strategy of bursting the hyperreal Insta-bubble by deliberately referring to the offline persona” (Mackay & Knox, 2024, p. 164). Holly McNish, a successful poet, resists the influencer label, but does post regularly on Instagram; the simulacrum is Baudrillard’s sense of digital simulations without referents (no basis or grounding in the real world. (My neighborhood Facebook group simulates my in-person conversations with real-life neighbors, but the map of Fortnite, a popular first-person shooter, doesn’t simulate a real place— it’s a simulation of place in and of itself without a real-world analogue.) Sarikaya argues that though Instapoetry relies in many ways on self-branding for its authenticity, pulling in the “real world” as content to seem relatable and real in an offline sense, 
this subversion of a hyperreal simulacrum is only successful if the interaction between poet and audience transcends the hyperreal medium into actual engagement in the ‘real’ world… more likely, the follower-consumer will not transgress the hyperreal state, remaining in the simulacrum of (online) intimacy. (Mackay & Knox, 2024, p. 178)
Sarikaya does hint at the ambivalences between McNish’s authenticities (her expression of political opinions, her transparency, her replies to comments, and so on). Specifically, McNish maintains privacy around her daughter and her home, something that many other influencers make central to their brand. Many traditional poets, hold their poetry out between themselves and their audience, speaking only to extreme personal matters and to interpretations of their own work, maintaining public personas that are a little less confessional, more wry, and depending less on the image of the author. 
Richard Siken, who had a cult following for his gay desire poetry collection Crush, had such a persona on Twitter before it was purchased and became X. Today, authors in the social media spaces are not going to leave their politics up to the critic to discern out strictly through the art anymore, though fans and users, certainly do critique the work and critique the author and the things that they say and do. However, authors like Siken understand that they are expected to speak out or to respond, and a “good” author puts forward their values themselves; they put forward their process themselves. Other writers put forward their ideas in a way that curates interest for their readings, and the merchandise that they put out is often times as ideological as it is referential to an original text of the author created. (Fans also do a massive amount of merchandise creation for cult texts like Mary Oliver’s “Wild Geese”, e.g.) Other writers in  reading #instagram discuss the confessional, the use of platform affordances to spread the literature like hashtags, the language of self-help as a confessional mechanism that brings readers and followers closer to them not by the aesthetics of language, but the relatability of the content. 
Behind the Scenes – Process as Content 
This is a problem for Seth Perlow, who centers the difference between art and content in his chapter of Expressive Networks, “On the Badness of Instagram Poetry.” “I reveal less about poetry itself than about the rhetorical norms that structure attributions of literary value today,” he opens, and many of his critiques lie in the mechanisms of self-promotion that I’m discussing in this essay—especially the purchase of self-disclosure and confessional by brands to link the poetic process with cosmetics and foods (Mackay & Knox, 2024, pp. 249). In one paid partnership, Instapoet Cleo Wade writes “in this place…. / the air is sweet / flowers are in bloom / there are smiles at every turn there is a feeling of home here.” Perlow draws out the association “that she wrote the poem during her stay—or perhaps that if you stay at a Fairmont Hotel, you too can receive such inspiration” (Mackay & Knox, 2024, pp. 252-3). This is an example of an obvious move away from being a Poet to being an Influencer – using poetry for the aesthetics and class-standings it connotes, rather than the merit of the aesthetics of the work or the true meanings of individual poems to the writer or its audience. 
Other writers market their expertise and hold workshops online, making a viable career by committing to literary activity and community that blurs the line between the reading audience and a sense of the audience as themselves being aspirational writers. This isn’t new for the writing community, though; it is only expressed more and expanded in the digital economy. Increasingly, literary magazine submission portals have turned to the tier model of digital marketing to make more money, providing options for $3 general submissions, $15 expedited review, $40 expedited review with detailed feedback from editors, etcetera. In this instance, we can turn to literary publishing, the litmag, and poetry itself as an original expression of the readership which is also the writer base. Litmags are an early example of platforms for user-generated content, alongside other kinds of writing in fanzines, newspaper columns, letters to the editor, and fora like the one in Ms. where readers would write in comments and submit found images of sexist advertising. Litmags might not necessarily go to great lengths to establish a readership, but now they can attain to a certain distributed readership through advertising and sponsored posts. 
The online creator who wants to self-promote, will often associate themselves with bookish aesthetics and images. Early in Rupi Kaur’s career, her book launch was commemorated with a series of images of her reading her own book in an Ottawa bookstore, and her ongoing social media presence involves her sitting around leaflets and pages from her books as well as her literally being surrounded by the poems on her own Instagram feed. In literary publishing, authors have to find a way to put themselves in proximity to their works, and the brand of the writer is increasingly playing a part in whose books get picked up by publishers. Especially in the mass market, books and authors with existing readerships and existing fan bases are more attractive to publishers because they bring a community with them rather than needing intense promotion from the beginning. It also signals to the company that the author themself will be comfortable promoting the work that they hope to publish.
Now, photos of Rupi Kaur around bookshelves and around loose pages of poems don't just signal that she is a writer in the general sense, but that she herself is writing the books that she is publishing, not an algorithm, not a ghostwriter. The proximity of a writer to the page is less interesting than watching a potter sculpt a piece of clay, and so the writing public has to find ways to verify the authenticity of the poem. Recently, literary magazines have begun to pick up this trend too. I watched one university literary magazine post a Reel (short-form video) on Instagram comparing the websites that readers and submitters see to a montage of the editors and students at the university in meetings reading with one another discussing indiscernible topics and poems with each other while music played over the footage. “This what you see versus what you don't see” indicates that there is a process of reading and evaluating and editing that signals the magazine's authenticity not as a content farm, but as a collaboration, as an effort of a community to put together something curated and original. These displays signal community at the same time that they signal attention and affection for the work that submitters and readers expect editors to do. Influencer creep is affecting the editors, not just the writers. Literary magazines are brands as much as writers and individual poets and novelists are.
Transition. The next two types of self-promotion facilitate not just brand loyalty, or the invitation for users to engage digitally, but also the invitation for users to participate physically and financially with brand engagement. These promotions can take a very overtly promotional tone (“I’m reading at AWP on Friday! Hope to see you there!”) or remain as digital events in and of themselves, such as Reddit AMAs (Ask Me Anythings) or TikTok Lives, and digital artifacts in and of themselves like NFTs, digital subscriptions, stickers, printables, or Snapchat filters. 
The Events – Performances as Content
Book talks and interviews are traditional means by which authors present themselves and participate in public discourse, because authors, especially those that have traditional cultural capital, are expected to serve as a kind of public figure who presents a check on the political and cultural landscape of our time. In the lead-up to this exam, I listened to old PBS interviews with Toni Morrison, between James Baldwin and Maya Angelou, between Baldwin and Nikki Giovanni, and so on. The book talk, which occurs on stage, the masterclass, which occurs between a writer and a class at a university, and the consultation, in which the prestigious writer meets individually with young writers over a packet of original work, are all traditional cultural capital expressions of the service that a writer will do to carry forward their persona. Today, however, all of these are remediated into the digital realm, as beef and call-outs on Twitter, as virtual events, and in even more content-like forms that disassociate the author from the mystique of the author and associate them with popular culture. 
It is interesting to which to the degree in which authors and creators interact with the comments sections and their [anti-]fans. The fandom for a book series might already be extremely robust across a variety of social media platforms, especially as works take on popular significance, and it often may not be necessary for a writer to establish direct and real contact with those fan communities. The poetry community on Twitter, for example, is comprised of the poets publishing the books and publishing pieces in journals, except for the Instapoets who publish their own books separately, but the fandoms for popular fiction titles don't necessarily need to interact directly with Sarah J. Maas. However, Sarah J. Maas is a high-value podcast guest and recently appeared on the Call Her Daddy podcast to announce her next two books in the A Court of Thorns and Roses series alongside traditional promotional routes. The creator and literary economies are intertwining: book releases have become social media events to which influencers are invited, either inviting members of the creator economy to position themselves as fans of literary works, or to position writers as fans of literature and as fans of popular culture.
When I studied Rupi Kaur’s Instagram presence, the tour and the meet and greet were incredibly important ways for Kaur to appeal to an existing community in order to self-promote. She hosted local readers as openers for her own readings, playing off of the protocols of the music industry to entrench her own personality as relatable to local readers and local reading communities. All of this work happens to different degrees of visibility and invisibility, and increasingly communities demand visibility of process as a way to verify reality and verify authenticity in the creators and the content that they consume and put their fandom into. 
The Artifact – Products as Content 
Jessica Pressman's Bookishness talks about the ways that works are remediated into items and aesthetic commodities, from books as things themselves that go on shelves and signal cultural capital AKA the fetish of the book, but also as the image of books being pasted onto brand merchandise. Jean Baudrillard’s concept of the hyperreal reappears in this book when Pressman describes how fake everything is, on page 87, even how all of the icons in the computer are based on bookish ideas and icons and images she cites Bolter and Grusin’s book Remediation and the ways that new media imitate old media, that this “fakery enables the digital.” “Fake books aren't new,” she says, but they play into the kitsch of being a reader (Pressman, 2020, (p. 89). I have my 2 shelves full of books that I have actually read, and my 3 shelves of books that I own but haven't read but still display. For both the reader and the writer bookishness is proof of something, of proximity to the cultural capital that books represent.
Conclusion
In the age of generative AI, traditional publishers are remediating some of these self-promotional instincts back into the print, as Bolter and Grusin suggest is part of the remediating modus operandi (1999). Authors and writers are making specific kinds of content to insulate themselves against accusations of being someone who uses AI; literary magazines and authors have remediated self-promotional elements of the Event, the Artifact, the Branded Self, and the Behind the Scenes back into traditional literary expression.  Self-disclosure appears in the print poetry collection as the Editor’s Note, the preface, the acknowledgements, and the ars poetica. Proof of Process, either as content marketing or as proof of effort in an anti-AI aesthetic field, is remediated into the print poetry collection as the process note in the computer-friendly literary magazines. The Event, as a virtual launch or a Zoom reading or webinar suggest, adapt the “Live” as a way for readers to engage from a distance. Merchandising has really taken off, with tote bags, hats, stickers, and pins becoming ways for publishers and literary magazines to represent themselves as iconic and institutional, more than just as a collective of editors printing a magazine artifact. 
This doesn’t mean that the art that these creators make, or that their expressions of their craft, can be reduced to being just content or written off as meaningless devoid of its publication and promotion on the platform. But in a platform economy (a digital exchange system in which goods, users, and their data are all commoditized), anything can be transformed into content. The remediation of art and expression into monetizable data performs a variety of social functions for the creator, and a range of economic functions for platforms and corporations. If authors won’t do it themselves, publishers can send ARCs (advance reader copies) to influencers to review their books. I recently met a woman who specifically makes content about “monster-fuckers,” a niche science fiction and speculative romance genre. Through an online portal, she commits via contract to receive ARCs, read them, and make a series of videos and posts on various social media platforms promoting the novels to her following. She has a curated influencer persona and a digital brand around reviewing this specific genre. Though the money is there, the precarity of her work lies in the amount of time she has to read the books she’s committed to, and her worry about whether she will be asked to review in the future if she provides negative reviews of any of the books she commits to reading. 
In “The Sociotechnical Architecture of Digital Labor,” Hector Postigo talks about all of the platform mechanisms that make play affordable to YouTube's algorithms and money making schemes, which in turn are distributed to creators, although creators also seek their own external forms of revenue (Postigo, 2016). There's revenue intrinsic to the platform and revenue external to the platform that the influencer or creator themselves attracts. There is an old form of using repeated publications of work to prove one’s merit to a publisher, like a manuscript with an acknowledgements section that shows that poems previously appeared in certain magazines and journals. These represent a certain kind of accumulated cultural capital from the previous validations of editors, but are not the same thing as content capital, which is generated simply by generating content on social media regularly enough and with enough engagement to prove that one “does the work,” which is not intrinsically a determining factor of aesthetic quality. If we’re going to think well moving forward, it's important and useful to distinguish practices intrinsic to Authorship today, like reading, writing, research, revision, and imagination, from practices intrinsic to the publishing economy. I want people to be authors, not just signal the social phenomenon of Authorship, and as so many of my references conclude, we need more media literacy to separate the two. 
 
 

 
In my second essay, I introduced Generative AI as “the ultimate bootstrapped text-technology,” meaning that GenAI as it currently exists is the implementation and culmination (if not weaponization) of dozens of pre-existing technologies and ideologies. Without cloud computing, the information paradigm that keeps the Internet and one’s own digital artifacts stored off-device in data centers, we wouldn’t have AI. Without Big Data or the platform economy, which devised a way to mine user-generated content and activity for information and market this information in datasets, we wouldn’t have AI. Without the Internet of Things, the networks of physical objects that we purchase and install in homes and in public to keep us connected and in control of our world, we wouldn’t have AI. Without the chat interface, and without decades of algorithm/mathematics research, we wouldn’t have Generative AI. Without the legal structures (and lack thereof) of American techno-capitalism, we wouldn’t have AI. AI, seen this way, is full-stack, both rhetorically and computationally: it’s “an accidental megastructure, one that we are building both deliberately and unwittingly” (Bratton, qtd. in Schintzler, 2025, pp. 209-211). To critique AI is to critique the entire system, the whole paradigm that smooths out our communications, our access to goods and services, and access to entertainment and community. This ideological construction is by design—“AI” is too big to fail. 
But to view AI this way also provides us access to history. To see AI as the sum of things that make it go gives us a place to start. To see AI as a product, not just in the sense of something to buy, but as an ideology and a technological feat that the technology class wants us to buy into, is the starting block for imagining a future, a world beyond AI. Carly Schintzler quotes N. Katherine Hayles in Expressive Networks, an edited collection about the spread of poetry in Web 2.0, and follows it with a comment that illuminates the liberatory potential of considering AI in its full-stack reality: 
“ ‘the hardware and software are active partners, facilitating and resisting, enabling and limiting, enacting and subverting.…’ The authors of the hardware and software that are active partners in the composition of the poem have their own ethical impetuses that are deployed through their use of the technology” (Kilbane, 2025, pp. 211). 
In the sources that I have read for this essay about Generative AI, it seems more and more that the problems that AI poses to “writing labor” are not about the threat that they might generate convincing content (though this is a threat in the context of scamming, phishing, and cybercrime). Instead, AI challenges our assumptions about what the work of writing means, recalling my previous investigations of ‘meaning’ as ‘making possible’. In a culture that can only think of writing in terms of its dollar value or as a measure of productivity, I don’t think letters will mean much: in the workplace and under capitalism, Generative AI absolutely seems like an inflection point in the production of language and text. 
However, I don’t believe that this grim outlook is the entire possible story. Instead, I will apply the mode of inquiry Stephen Ramsay argues for in Reading Machines, ‘pataphysics (the apostrophe at the beginning is part of its spelling), which “is at once the product of both scientific and artistic investigation” as “the science of imaginary solutions” which “refuses to see the relativity of perspective as a barrier to knowledge” (Ramsay, 2011, pp. 20-21). ‘Pataphysics emerges from absurdism and surrealism and precedes the Oulipo, who were concerned with potential amid constraint, the exact mindset with which I wish to approach Generative AI. Applying ‘pataphysics to “writing labor” is to ask what potential is there in the constraints we now face in our writing lives, what new ways of writing, what rhetorics, what poetries, will emerge in response to the phenomenon of AI. 
Thesis. 
So, I concede that Generative AI is an inflection point for the labor of writing, but imagine the inflection in the sense of the word that arrives to us from grammar. In English, the word ‘develop’ for example can be inflected by an -s to actualize, by -ed to put in the past, by -ing to be present with. Each inflection doesn’t change the root meaning of ‘develop.’ I argue that Generative AI is this kind of inflection on the work of writing—writing has not changed its root meaning but inflects our experience of writing (and by extension, of reading) by actualizing it in a distinct way from computational writing/code inflected writing, or how the typewriter and printing press inflected writing in their own ways. GenAI also inflects how we narrativize writing in public by creating a new sense of the ‘past’ and a new sense of the ‘present,’ a new  sense of pre- and post-(AI). To the literate public, GenAI is seen as new potential or as new constraints, challenging the assumptions we arrive to blank pages and to texts with. 
To say so plainly, I hope to show that Generative AI inflects (1) our justifications of writing as “the thing humans do,” (2) our writing processes as inscription changes, (3) our paradigm of words and expressions as united with some sort of reference, (4) our understanding of culture as something that is reserved to us based on our relationships with experience. My understandings of the way GenAI inflects these aspects of the work of writing (which is the work of culture) are based on N. Katherine Hayles’ media-specific theory in Writing Machines, Stephen Ramsay’s literary criticism in Reading Machines, Leif Weatherby’s philosophy of AI in Language Machines, and Lev Manovich and Emanuelle Arielli’s collaborative investigations in Artificial Aesthetics. 
I’ll draw on textual evidence from two anthologies of work. The first is OUTPUT, a collection by Lillian-Yvonne Bertram and Nick Montfort of computer-generated text from 1953 to 2023. The second is Ensemble Park: A Journal of Human + Computer Writing, a periodical curated by Kyle Booten and Katy Ilonka Gero, who are both creative writers and researchers interested in human-computer interaction; they both make computational writing as well. Ensemble Park has the most robust explications of writing with generative AI by the authors compared to any other publication, featuring both a process note by the author and a follow-up interview between the editors of EP and the contributor. The pieces in Ensemble Park use computers to generate text, but they also require that the human behind that productive aesthetic activity justify and explain their concept and their narrative reasons for endeavoring to create with the mechanisms of a computer in ways that I don't believe we demand of typewritten books. As an aside, Kyle Booten’s book Gyms is an art project exemplifying the spirit of ‘pataphysics: it leverages LLMs to execute his conviction that generative and automatic writing systems should make human writing (the cognitive processes of writing and drafting) more difficult, not easier. Booten applies LLMs to constrain his writing and foster new potential literature, but he does so in an designed act of refusal (Zong & Matias, 2024) to let the ideologies of Generative AI—efficiency, automated writing, the offloading of labor— take over the creative process. 
1.     Writing as something Humans do 
Both Lev Manovich in Artificial Aesthetics and Leif Weatherby in Language Machines interrogate the paradigm of “writing as something humans do” and “art as something only humans can make” in different ways. But they interrogate this assumption on a variety of grounds. 
Weatherby attacks that question from a pretty practical standpoint, which is remainder humanism, which is our humanistic self-definition in contrast what the machine can or can't do. In the closing of that gap, the automation of things we define as human, is becomes an intrinsic threatening of who we are, because we have not defined what we are as humans intrinsically, only defined “humanity” through a list of unique functions. Leaf Weatherby in language machines resists remainder humanism, “a humanism without a theory or doctrine of what is human, in which humanity is remaindered… defined by technology’s creep, but only negatively” (Weatherby, 2025, p. 34). Later he will say that intention is what makes poetry into poems, then as Weatherby cites Allison Parrish in later in the book, “Large Language Models can only write poetry.” Parrish distinguishes what poetry is from what a poem is: poetry, defined by William Carlos Williams, is “new form dealt with as a reality in itself,” but a poem is “an intentional arrangement resulting from some action” that “unites poetry with an intention” (qtd. in Weatherby, 2025, p. 125). Poetry is a way of working with language that a large language can do well, but it can't write a poem because only intention can form language into a poem. 
Manovich approaches this question a little bit differently, through the investigation of what it really means to make. He takes apart many myths of creativity that actually find their roots in the enlightenment, as well as the definitions of Art. Both authors arrive in this place where we can begin to speak specifically about what Language models really do, compared to what humans do, and find that while we do them expressively, computers do them because they are made to and told to, and thereby can never say anything true to themselves, only execute classifications and generations of examples of classified objects (categorization and genre) and subjects (data-informed descriptions and data-informed recommendations). 
2.     Writing Differently 
N. Kate Hayles talks about digital writing as inscription, because of the materiality of the computer. Just because the digital is electronic inscription doesn’t mean that there isn’t “material changes that can be read as marks,” as Hayles defines inscription. “Telegraphy thus counts;” she says, “it sends structured electronic pulses through a wire” and turns these encoded electronic signals into analogue signals “associated with marks on paper” (Hayles, 2002, p. 24). In the 20th century alone, letter forming has diversified series of scratches of ink or graphite on paper, to metal letters punched or pressed through ink/tape onto paper, to buttons that activate lasers and electronics at the press of a series of buttons. Then, new encryptions of typed letters could execute longer texts, as OUTPUT shows in its sections on  including computational writing that transformed certain letters (code) into executions much longer than the original code. What Generative AI seems to do is represent a new kind of inscription that multiplies the letter-forming process from a semantically-encoded set of letters (a prompt, instead of a file of computer code) into a much longer semantic document (the generated text, both in code and in plain English).
I want to draw these texts near to Critical Making in the Age of AI (Johnson & Salter, 2025), too, which provides many exercises that involve a certain kind of writing that generates more texts and readings than any one original creator can have a hand in, like Twine stories (hypertext choice-based narratives), games (with many rooms and interactions that provide a series of potential decisions, but do not activate without the player, Twitter Bots (which execute as many combinations of possible texts based on code), and GIFs which contain a reference but then are remixed into completely disparate textual narratives, situations and meanings. All of these different texts expand the sense of the “written” to the code-space, where all of a sudden text can execute commands and trigger reactions, not just be perceived as static letter-forms on the text.
The central ambivalence of Generative AI as a writing technology is the ambivalence between the digital and the material, between physical labor and mental labor. As I’ve taken the course that reflects the text Critical Making in the Age of AI, different iterations of digital texts from the selfie, comic, gif/flipbook, to the bitsy game, the bot, distant reading, to the prompt exist along a spectrum of automatability and templatability by generative AI. A BlueSky bot could be written in Tracery extremely easily, or one could generate a much longer list of options for a mad-libs template to draw on using generative AI. On the other hand, the Bitsy game builder remains a pretty tactile and inaccessible interface for generative AI. Even an agentic browser would take much longer than I to write out the blocks that make up the textual, visual and procedural components of a Bitsy game. For evidence of the materiality of writing in the age of generative AI, I turn to N. Katherine Hayles. Even as her literary criticism was challenged by the emergence of hyperlinked electronic literature, her convictions about the materiality of the hypertext remained intact, as she describes in Writing Machines. 
The variance of the kinds of codes that can be written to generate computer-generated texts is found across OUTPUT. The variations most like a Mad Libs, where certain nouns are filled in procedurally from a list provided to the program, are in the “Bots” section and the section on “Reporting,” which lists several real-world applications of generative writing for social good, like the BBC’s coverage of the UK general election in 2019. “To ensure accuracy and adherence to BBC style, the news organization went with a rule-based approach that used templates, eschewing large language models” (Bertram & Montfort, 2024, p. 330). Other formulaic programs are much more defamiliarizing, and less about reporting truth to the public than, in one instance, “Brute-Forcing” “all true assertions” in the form of syllogisms, which total to around 15,000 pages of material in the style of “Aleph is a type of letter / Letter is a type of character / Therefore, aleph is a type of letter” (Bertram & Montfort, 2024, p. 324).
3.     Reading Differently
N. Katherine Hayles talks about the profound changes to her reading experience and her literary criticism by the experience of first- and second-generation electronic literature. The interface differences between the book and the work of electronic literature really challenged the paradigms of literary criticism that Hayles entered the works with—what was literary criticism to make of the hyperlink and the layered image-text? What was literary criticism to make of the choices that readers were able to make in Patchwork Girl?
Hayles talks about serving on Espen Aarseth’s defense committee, when he was beginning to put together what would become Cybertexts his book on ergodic literature, in which readers make narrative choices in games and hypertext fiction. Aarseth’s book, which came out in 1997, interrogates computer generated poetry and prose of its own time, and just like Reading Machines, OUTPUT, and other works on this list, raises the traditional history of chance operations and randomizing in the production of meaningful cultural texts. Steven Ramsay also speaks about the I-Ching and thinks about the divinatory text as a part of physical narrative device that enacts an imaginative reading through the operations of chance and into associations and possibilities not directly inscribed in the reference guide the I-Ching provides for its many combinations. Steven Ramsay calls the I-Ching an algorithm, the same way that a lipogram, a text that must be written with or without certain letters, is its own algorithm that yields only certain kinds of texts. Algorithms, as Lev Manovich talks about, both read and write objects and subjects, and Hayles’s sense of the material and having been transformed by their readings point to this same reality.
OUTPUT and Ensemble Parks specifically help us realize the differences between works generated by large language models and works generated by computer code. In old computer systems the code itself had to template the writing, as is evident in assignments like the tracery and Twitter bots found in the critical making book there are a limited number or a templated, predictable templates for the tweets and texts and sentences and words generated by computer programs and Twitter bots in tracery dot JS but feasibly the reproducibility of text with a large language model has no specified limitation numerically that can be counted by careful examination of code. Instead the limit to the prompter is the limit of one's own imagination, according to the marketing, or the limited number of tokens you have to spend generating content, according to the market. This is an important space to remember that generative AI retains the rights to texts created with AI unless someone has an agreement otherwise. The prompts that we use to generate texts are fed into AI as a process of continual retraining, unless otherwise stated and protected. As such the writing that we do is absorbed into the writing that AI does. AI also takes the prompts at face value, in a similar way to how Steven Ramsey's quotes Christian Bach in reading machines when he says that questions presume a specific range of answers possible answers.
It’s important that while the existence of Generative AI challenges the assumptions we bring to texts we read, like that a human sat and typed it personally, we also bring correct understandings of the ways that Generative AI itself is also constrained, in opposition to the assumption and the marketing that tries to convince us that Generative AI is an “everything machine.” A case study of these constraints specifically in the realm of poetry is Melanie Walsh, Anna Preus, and Elizabeth Gronski’s work on the style of ChatGPT’s poetry across poetry forms. Using computational linguistics tools to compare word frequency across a corpus of poems generated by GPT 3.5 and 4 with a corpus of poems from the Poetry Foundation and the Academy of American Poets, they found that “GPT models are much formulaic and constrained than human-authored poetry,” they write. This research suggests that GenAI writing is much more akin to the writing found in OUTPUT, which follows programming and templating, until it is applied by an artist. 
Ensemble Park asked Berkeley German professor and theorist Hannes Bajohr, the contributor of a short story generated by a fine-tuned LLM, “what does it feel like to write with this model?” His answer foreshadows the language Weatherby is searching for in Language Machines about what a computer-writer really does – draft, or template? Bajohr says, “I don’t think this experience has impacted my normal, “manual” writing at all. It is just too different.… Using an LLM as I have is a much more distanced, at times almost spectatorial act that has little to do with the often-painful process of putting words on the screen.… I don’t see the machine as the agent of writing. It does not appear to me as an artificial author. Rather, there is a ‘will of the text’ at work.” He analogizes: a surfboard isn’t an agent, and a horse is more than a mere tool. “In the end, LLMs may be a third thing for which one would have to find a practice and a name” (p. 18). Weatherby approximates that as LLMs (which he prefers to extend as “large literature machines”) draft or ghostwrite rather than write (p. 200). There are multiple inflection points in the history of writing an inscription technologies, as and N. Katherine Hales points out in her book Writing Machines from 2002, which is written as a third-person memoir that investigates new media theory during the transition between the first and second generations of hypertext literature and electronic literature. But what N. Katherine Hayles does point out is the shifts between handwriting and movable type and the typewriter and now the computer and now image text on the computer all have their own sense of inflection upon what it means to craft a literary text at a given time. 
4.     Culture, Now
AI also inflects our awareness of culture as a made thing, as a possibly automated thing. Hayles reflects on the typewriter and the materiality of all of these works of electronic literature, and specifically about how the typewriter isn't an inscription technology the way that a pencil is, but that it turns the inscription from a concept a conceptual ideal into an ideal of Letters. An algorithmic criticism in Reading Machines, Ramsay calls for ‘pataphysics in literary criticism which are attentive to the potentialities of literature and the potentialities of language rather than to their limitations or, better said, to their limitations as potentials for new kinds of thought in the traditions of the Oulipo. Structured thought, whether structured by a physical writing constraint or by a computational writing framework or algorithm, sees something like a lipogram as an algorithm for new kinds of writing. Culture, like real culture, is about a relationship between the expression and the real, which an AI doesn't have and can only simulate.  Hayles in Writing Machines talks about how reading early electronic literature, that is, works with texts and images that are hyperlinked and layered, changed the way that she read those works, because there were new modes of inscription beyond just perceiving the text as a story or world that text encodes with language, there were also other components of craft that these like new ways of communicating, suggested or made possible new ways of moving through a text than turning a page or flipping back and forth through a book. These senses of reading being changed also changes our sense of everything we read; these senses of writing changing also changes our awarenesses when we arrive at the page, and we cultivate – we cultivate-- differently. 
 
But what changes about the work of a writer now; is it the presumption that what might be read might be written by a machine?  We are already being read by machines, and we are already being written upon by machines (by algorithms and by automated mail that we receive from the courthouse for jury duty, by our professors and universities, etc.) This sort of goes back to the platform position, concepts of looking at a text as legible to whom and to what end? A text is not just legible to my mom and dad or my friends and followers, but also to the algorithm, and also to the advertiser over and against the sense of an intended reader. It's not just the meaning of the text or the text itself as potentially being read as good or bad, but also as whether it is real, whether it's an authentic expression of a person or generated as a way to cope or to, or to overcome a deficiency or an inefficiency. N. Katherine Hayles and the writers I have mentioned, though inadequately, above, hold the simulacrum and the material in tandem, something we must do to imagine a future in which we exist and where the existence of AI has been adequately reconciled. 
Conclusion.
To close, the historical perspective of OUTPUT suggests that while Generative AI is not an inflection in the work of writing intrinsically, it is an inflection in the social sense of what it means to write— in the current moment, it represents rupture and change in what the mass majority of the writing public might understand by the meaning of the words “to write.” The historical and technological explanations provided by the introductory texts in OUTPUT remind us that Generative AI is itself an expression of previous forms writing automation, exploitation and alienation from capital, and from the logics which seek to divorce the writer from their work. This is a labor issue. Generative AI threatens to turn intellectual labor into a managerial task rather than an unalienated one in which the writer is at in touch literally with the product and fruit of the work of writing, in which they have as N. Katherine Hayles says, by writing having thought differently, and by thinking differently having written differently by the by their hand by touch. All of this talk of touch does remind us that the digital is material and Hayles opens riding machines with a reflection on the materiality of these computers. What generative AI continues and really reveals in its newness is the explicit attempts to conceal the material labor of writing from the production of new texts.
